### Minimum editorial standard


Every article must deliver a substantive, coherent, original treatment of the named topic. Most ordinary articles should aim for approximately 1,800–3,200 words; demanding technical, state-profile, legal, enterprise economics and major crop articles may require 3,000–5,000 words. These are quality guides, not mechanical targets. Do not write filler to hit the word count. The aggregate manuscript should become a large reference work through breadth and real depth. If a topic is inherently narrow, explain it precisely rather than padding it.


Use a clear order suited to the topic. Articles should ordinarily cover the following only where relevant: what the subject is; its terminology and boundaries; where and under what conditions it applies; production biology or technical mechanisms; relevant Indian and global contexts; variants and alternatives; resource requirements; step-by-step process descriptions in prose; required skills and equipment; yields and quality drivers; input requirements; labour and capital; marketing and buyer requirements; regulatory compliance; safety and environment; economics and risks; common mistakes; reasons for failure; what is not yet known; how to validate locally; and references. Do not force every section into every article. The topic-specific article brief and its source evidence should determine the contents.


### TTS and combined book requirements


The canonical article body must read well aloud. Prefer paragraphs and meaningful Markdown headings. Avoid large tables, nested bullets, dense symbols, emoji, raw HTML, decorative dividers, code blocks, and visual-only explanations. A very small list may be acceptable when it is the most clear form, but prose is preferred. Never hide critical advice in an image or graphic. Explain formulas verbally, and if a mathematical representation materially helps on the website, pair it with a clear plain-language narration and ensure the book export does not leave unreadable symbolic clutter. Technical units and scientific names must be used correctly and explained when unfamiliar.


Unlike a generic business blog, agricultural content needs numbers. Use numeric yield, input, rainfall, price and cost claims only where the source and time period are available. Write units and assumptions clearly in the text. Do not create unsourced annual profits per acre. Include sourced ranges only when comparable definitions and geographies justify comparison. Explain uncertainty verbally. Do not claim that a crop is inherently profitable; profitability depends on location, water, soil, labour, crop duration, yield, quality, farm size, market access, realized price, cost accounting, timing, risk and alternative uses of land and capital.


### A high bar for agricultural instructions


Recommendations about pesticide, veterinary medicines, fertilizer dose, seed treatment, feed formulation, irrigation, greenhouse controls or food safety can cause financial loss, crop damage or harm to people, animals and ecosystems. Only state technical prescriptions when backed by current, credible, crop- and region-relevant evidence and product labels/rules where applicable. Do not invent pesticide mixtures, doses, veterinary treatment protocols, off-label drug uses, withdrawal periods, fertilizer prescriptions or universal irrigation schedules. When a diagnosis is uncertain, guide readers toward a local KVK, state agricultural university, qualified agronomist, licensed veterinarian, official advisory or accredited laboratory. Mention personal protective equipment, pre-harvest intervals, resistance management, water and pollinator protection where relevant. Do not promote banned or unregistered inputs.
