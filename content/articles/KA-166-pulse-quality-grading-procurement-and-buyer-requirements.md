# Pulse Quality, Grading, Procurement and Buyer Requirements

The commercial valorization of grain legumes across wholesale terminal mandis, industrial processing hubs, and institutional procurement channels depends upon rigorous adherence to physical, chemical, and sanitary quality parameters. In contemporary agricultural trade, the simplistic assumption that all pulses of a given botanical class are fungible commodities has been completely dismantled. Commercial dhal millers, export consolidators, institutional procurement agencies like the National Agricultural Cooperative Marketing Federation of India, and organized retail brands evaluate pulse consignments through strict quantitative metrics governing physical purity, grain size uniformity, moisture thresholds, defect counts, milling recovery potential, and chemical safety compliance. A farmer or aggregator who delivers grain failing to satisfy these stringent statutory and industrial benchmarks faces severe price deductions, consignment rejection at mandi gates, or complete exclusion from lucrative institutional price support mechanisms. Consequently, mastering quality grading frameworks codified under national food safety statutes and understanding the mechanical kinetics of dhal milling recovery represents the decisive commercial link between farm production and profitable market realization.

The regulatory architecture governing pulse commerce in India is bifurcated into mandatory statutory food safety parameters enforced by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India and voluntary commercial quality grading standards administered by the Directorate of Marketing and Inspection under the Agricultural Produce (Grading and Marking) Act, universally recognized as AGMARK. The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India operates under the Food Safety and Standards (Food Products Standards and Food Additives) Regulations, establishing mandatory, non-negotiable legal thresholds for all whole pulses, split pulses, and derived flours entering human food chains. These statutory regulations legally cap moisture content between twelve and fourteen percent depending on the specific legume species, mandate that foreign inorganic matter like sand, stones, and gravel cannot exceed zero point five percent by weight, and restrict organic foreign matter to a maximum of one percent. Damaged, discolored, and insect-bored grains are restricted below two to three percent, while weeviled grain counts are capped at a maximum of one to two percent by count. Furthermore, statutory food laws establish a strict zero-tolerance prohibition against the artificial polishing or cosmetic coloring of dhal using harmful non-permitted chemical dyes—such as metanil yellow, auramine, or lead chromate—which were historically employed by unscrupulous traders to disguise low-grade or rain-damaged split pigeon pea as premium yellow tur dal. In contrast, AGMARK grading provides a commercial quality certification framework that stratifies pulses into commercial grades—such as Special, Good, Fair, and Ordinary—based on thousand-grain test weight, seed coat integrity, cotyledon color brilliance, and dimensional uniformity, serving as the essential quality benchmark for interstate contracts and international export consignments managed by the Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority.

For industrial dhal millers, the primary economic determinant governing raw pulse procurement is the dhal milling recovery percentage. Grain legumes cannot be consumed directly as whole dry seeds; they must undergo industrial primary processing to remove the fibrous outer seed coat (pericarp) and split the two internal cotyledons into clean, ready-to-cook dhal. The milling recovery percentage measures the finished weight of clean, split dhal obtained from a given mass of raw, cleaned whole grain. In traditional village chakki mills or outdated commercial mills, dehusking efficiency is notoriously poor, with recovery percentages languishing between sixty-five and seventy-two percent, as significant portions of valuable cotyledon starches are pulverized into low-value powder (churi or chuni) and fine broken fragments. Modern industrial dhal mills utilizing advanced carborundum emery rollers, aspirating air classifiers, vibratory multi-deck graders, and color-sorting optical cameras achieve dehusking efficiencies exceeding ninety-five percent, elevating finished split dhal recovery rates to seventy-eight to eighty-five percent. Commercial millers pay premium prices for raw pulse consignments characterized by uniform bold seed diameter, spherical symmetry, absence of mechanical micro-cracking, and tight seed coat adherence, as heterogeneous grain sizes pass unevenly through milling roller clearances, causing excessive breakage of large grains while leaving small grains unhusked.

Pre-milling grain conditioning represents a critical technological phase that directly governs dhal recovery kinetics. In raw pulses, particularly pigeon pea, black gram, and green gram, the seed coat is bound tenaciously to the underlying cotyledon surfaces by a natural, mucilaginous layer rich in pectin, arabinogalactans, and insoluble hemicellulose. Milling unconditioned grain results in severe abrasion and high cotyledon breakage. Industrial millers execute precise pre-milling conditioning protocols combining sun drying, moisture tempering, and edible vegetable oil application. Clean whole grains are gently scarified by passing them through a light emery roller to create micro-scratches in the seed coat, followed by thoroughly blending the grain with one to two percent refined edible vegetable oil, such as mustard oil or linseed oil. The oiled grain is conditioned in open drying yards for twenty-four to forty-eight hours, during which the oil penetrates the seed coat micro-scratches and chemically softens the internal pectin-mucilage layer. Subsequent water spraying and resting induce differential expansion and contraction between the fibrous seed coat and the starchy cotyledons, causing the hull to loosen cleanly and detach from the cotyledon during subsequent passage through dehusking rollers with minimal friction and virtually zero cotyledon shattering.

Institutional procurement under the government Minimum Support Price framework operates through rigorous Fair Average Quality specifications that dictate whether a farmer's harvest is purchased or rejected at state procurement centers. Procuring agencies, led by the National Agricultural Cooperative Marketing Federation of India and state civil supplies corporations under the Price Support Scheme, enforce standardized Fair Average Quality parameters for chickpea, pigeon pea, green gram, black gram, and lentil. To qualify for full Minimum Support Price payment, delivered pulse consignments must strictly contain not more than twelve percent moisture, maximum two percent foreign matter, maximum three percent damaged or discolored grains, maximum four percent slightly damaged grains, maximum three percent immature or shriveled grains, and maximum one percent weeviled grains. If a consignment exceeds the twelve percent moisture threshold, procurement officers will reject the lot or mandate on-site re-drying, as storing high-moisture pulses in public buffer stocks triggers rapid fungal decay and pulse beetle multiplication that threatens national food reserves.

Commercial buyers and organized retail brands enforce additional culinary and functional specifications that influence premium pricing. Chief among these is the cooking time and water absorption index. In retail consumer kitchens, consumers demand dhal that cooks to a smooth, creamy, homogenous consistency within twenty to twenty-five minutes of boiling or pressure cooking, without leaving hard, unsoftened cotyledon fragments known as hard-cooking defects. Hard-to-cook defects develop when pulses are stored for extended periods under high temperature and humidity, triggering phytase enzyme degradation and the migration of divalent calcium and magnesium ions into cell walls, where they cross-link with pectin to form insoluble pectate barriers that resist water penetration during boiling. By implementing disciplined on-farm drying to ten percent moisture, investing in hermetic storage, avoiding synthetic pesticide contamination, and cleaning grain to eliminate stones and chaff, pulse producers ensure that their harvest complies with statutory food safety laws, satisfies institutional Fair Average Quality norms, and commands maximum premium valuations from commercial dhal millers.
