# When Millets Are Agronomically Suitable but Commercially Difficult

A central paradox in contemporary agricultural development is the stark divergence between agronomic suitability and commercial profitability. From an ecological and physiological standpoint, millets represent the ideal crop choice for vast expanses of India's rainfed agrarian landscape. They thrive in shallow, rocky soils where other crops wither, survive prolonged dry spells through C4 photosynthetic efficiency and osmotic adjustment, demand less than half the water consumed by fine cereals, require minimal synthetic chemical fertilizers, and produce grain packed with essential micronutrients and dietary fiber. Yet, despite decades of state advocacy and recent international promotional campaigns, millions of dryland farmers consistently abandon millets whenever the opportunity arises, opting instead to cultivate water-intensive paddy, sugarcane, hybrid maize, cotton, or commercial pulses. This persistent refusal to adopt or expand millet acreage is neither irrational nor stubborn; it represents an economically rational response to systemic commercial bottlenecks, missing processing infrastructure, highly illiquid spot markets, labor-intensive field operations, and acute revenue disparities.

The foremost structural barrier rendering millets commercially unviable in many rural districts is the absolute absence of decentralized primary processing infrastructure. While naked grains like wheat, rice, and maize can be threshed, bagged, and sold immediately at any local village mandi or cooperative, husked minor millets—kodo, kutki, foxtail, proso, and barnyard millet—cannot enter human food supply chains until their abrasive, silica-rich outer husks are mechanically removed. If a smallholder farmer in an isolated block produces two tonnes of raw kodo millet, but the nearest specialized centrifugal dehuller or rubber-roller sheller is located one hundred and fifty kilometers away in a distant district center, the farmer cannot process the harvest domestically without weeks of exhausting manual hand-pounding. Consequently, the farmer is forced to sell raw unhulled grain to predatory village middlemen at depressed distress prices of fifteen to twenty rupees per kilogram. Transporting small, fragmented volumes over long distances to commercial mills is economically prohibitive, as freight charges consume the entire anticipated margin. Until primary processing units, aspirators, and destoners are established within a ten-to-fifteen-kilometer radius of farming clusters, cultivating minor millets remains an economic trap for resource-poor growers.

The second commercial hurdle stems from thin, fragmented, and volatile market channels. In sharp contrast to wheat, paddy, and oilseeds, which enjoy standardized grading, transparent open auctions, and massive liquidity across hundreds of regulated Agricultural Produce Market Committee mandis, millets are traded in thin, unregulated spot markets characterized by extreme price discovery opacity. Because total traded volumes in any single rural market are modest, spot prices are easily crashed by minor seasonal supply fluctuations. A localized bumper harvest of little millet or foxtail millet often causes local farm-gate prices to collapse by forty to sixty percent within two weeks, as local buyers quickly saturate their modest storage capacities. Furthermore, while the central government announces Minimum Support Prices for sorghum, pearl millet, and finger millet, physical government procurement operations remain concentrated in a handful of progressive states like Karnataka and Odisha. In states where statutory procurement is absent, declared support prices exist merely on paper, leaving dryland growers completely exposed to unbuffered commodity price volatility.

The third economic reality constraining millet adoption is the insurmountable physical yield ceiling when compared to responsive commercial crops. Under rainfed conditions on marginal soils, high-yielding millet varieties typically yield one point two to one point eight tonnes of clean grain per hectare. In contrast, modern hybrid maize cultivated under identical rainfall conditions reliably produces four to six tonnes per hectare, while irrigated paddy yields four to five tonnes. Even when millets command a modest price premium per kilogram over coarse feed grains, the overwhelming volumetric advantage of maize or rice generates vastly higher gross cash revenues per unit of land. For smallholder farm families subsisting on one or two hectares, gross cash revenue is the paramount determinant of household survival, dictating their ability to purchase food staples, pay children's school fees, and settle medical emergencies. Recommending low-yielding millets to a farmer who possesses access to even a modest borewell or seasonal canal irrigation is economically unviable, as high-value vegetables, pulses, or fine cereals deliver three to five times higher net seasonal returns per hectare.

Labor dynamics and operational drudgery constitute the fourth major commercial impediment. Cultivating millets involves high manual labor inputs during critical operational windows, especially weed management, bird scaring, and harvesting. Because young millet seedlings emerge slowly, fields require multiple manual hand-weedings to prevent fast-growing broadleaf weeds from smothering the crop. As reproductive panicles emerge and mature, their exposed, nutrient-dense seedheads attract massive flocks of granivorous birds, including sparrows, parakeets, and weavers. In many rural tracts, farmers must spend twelve hours a day in the field for four consecutive weeks shouting, banging metal plates, and operating sling shots to prevent migratory birds from devouring up to thirty percent of the standing crop. Furthermore, because uneven ripening and fragile panicles prevent the easy operation of standard large combine harvesters, harvesting is predominantly conducted manually using hand sickles. In rural regions experiencing severe agricultural labor shortages, where daily farm wages have escalated to three hundred and fifty to five hundred rupees per day, paid labor costs consume fifty to seventy percent of the total gross value of the harvested grain.

Storage vulnerability and rapid post-processing deterioration introduce severe commercial risks for farmers, aggregators, and small-scale food enterprises. While whole, unhulled millet grains encased in their silica-rich husks possess legendary storage longevity, resisting insect pests for years in traditional underground storage pits, the situation reverses violently once grains are dehulled or milled into flour. The high concentration of unsaturated lipids within the millet germ, combined with potent endogenous lipase enzymes, triggers rapid lipid oxidation. Within seven to fifteen days after milling at tropical ambient temperatures, dehulled grains and flours develop foul-smelling volatile aldehydes and bitter free fatty acids, rendering them unpalatable and unmarketable. Processors and rural retail entrepreneurs who lack expensive nitrogen-flushing vacuum-packaging machinery or refrigerated cold-storage warehouses face high inventory spoilage losses, creating extreme commercial reluctance to hold processed millet stocks.

Finally, cultural dietary displacement and decades of food subsidies have deeply eroded domestic rural consumption demand. Under the Public Distribution System, rural families across dryland India have enjoyed subsidized or free access to polished white rice and refined wheat for generations. Over decades, this policy transformed rural food preferences, institutionalizing fine cereals as prestigious dietary staples while stigmatizing millets as coarse, bitter grains associated with poverty and social backwardness. Even within dryland farming villages where millets were traditionally cultivated, farming households frequently prefer eating soft, sweet white rice over dense, heavy millet flatbreads. Consequently, commercial farmers cannot rely on local village consumption to absorb their harvests and are forced to depend entirely on distant, fickle urban wellness consumers. When evaluating agricultural investments, farm managers must recognize that agronomic adaptation to drought is meaningless without commercial market access, decentralized processing facilities, assured institutional procurement, and viable labor economics. Until these systemic market failures are resolved through coordinated supply chain investments, contract farming partnerships, and sustained public procurement, millets will remain agronomically superior yet commercially precarious crops across the drylands of India.
