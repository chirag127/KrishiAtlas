# Field Pea and Other Cool-Season Grain Legumes

Field pea, classified botanically as Pisum sativum subspecies arvense, represents one of the most productive, versatile, and high-yielding cool-season rabi grain legumes cultivated across northern, central, and eastern India. Unlike horticultural garden peas (Pisum sativum variety hortense), which are bred for succulent sweetness and harvested immature as tender green table vegetables, field peas are cultivated specifically for dry, mature, starchy seeds that are threshed, split into yellow cotyledons, and consumed as dhal, flour, or processed savory snacks. Thriving under the bright, crisp winter sunshine of the Indo-Gangetic Plains and the Bundelkhand plateau, field pea exhibits an aggressive growth habit, high harvest index, and superior biological nitrogen-fixation capability. Simultaneously, the broader spectrum of secondary cool-season grain legumes—most notably faba bean (Vicia faba) and grasspea (Lathyrus sativus)—occupies specialized agro-ecological niches characterized by heavy clay floodplains, extreme winter chill, and marginal moisture regimes that defeat other commercial pulses.

The agronomic management of field pea requires strict alignment with winter seasonal temperatures and soil drainage characteristics. Field pea thrives in cool, relatively dry climates with optimum ambient temperatures ranging between fifteen and twenty-two degrees Celsius. While the crop requires moderate moisture during vegetative branching and pod elongation, standing water or saturated soil conditions lasting longer than twenty-four hours induce rapid root asphyxiation, collar rot caused by Sclerotium rolfsii, and root rot complexes caused by Fusarium and Rhizoctonia species. Soils must be well-drained loams, sandy clay loams, or silt loams with a neutral reaction between potential of hydrogen 6.5 and 7.5. Seedbed preparation requires one deep summer plowing to eliminate perennial weed rhizomes, followed by two harrowings and light planking to produce a firm, friable, uncompacted seedbed. The optimum sowing window spans the second fortnight of October through the first week of November across Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, and Haryana. Sowing delayed into late November exposes the vegetative canopy to winter fog and dampness that trigger catastrophic foliar diseases, while pushing the critical pod-filling stage into the hot, desiccating winds of late February and March, which truncate seed development.

Sowing operations and biological seed inoculation govern plant stand density and nutrient capture in field pea. Normal-seeded varieties require an optimal seed rate of seventy-five to eighty kilograms per hectare, whereas bold-seeded cultivars require ninety to one hundred kilograms per hectare to establish a target plant population of three hundred and thirty thousand plants per hectare at a row spacing of thirty centimeters and plant-to-plant spacing of ten centimeters. Sowing depth must be maintained between four and five centimeters in moist soil. Because field peas enter into symbiotic mutualism with Rhizobium leguminosarum biovar viciae, certified seed should be treated with host-specific bio-inoculants combined with phosphate-solubilizing bacteria at twenty-five grams per kilogram of seed, preceded by chemical seed dressing with thiram or carbendazim at two grams per kilogram. Basal fertilizer application requires a modest starter dose of twenty kilograms of nitrogen and forty to fifty kilograms of phosphorus pentoxide per hectare drilled below the seed at planting. In phosphorus-deficient soils, phosphorus application stimulates extensive root branching and fuels the high adenosine triphosphate requirements of the nitrogenase enzyme within active pink nodules. One or two light irrigations—applied at the pre-flowering branching stage and the early pod-filling stage—elevate grain yields by thirty to fifty percent without causing excessive vegetative lodging.

The paramount pathological constraint in field pea production is powdery mildew, caused by the obligate biotrophic ascomycete fungus Erysiphe pisi. The disease manifests during reproductive pod maturation under weather characterized by dry, sunny days, cool humid nights, and heavy morning dews. Initial symptoms appear as faint, circular, white powdery spots on lower leaves, which rapidly coalesce into a continuous, floury, talcum-powder-like fungal mycelium covering upper leaf surfaces, stems, tendrils, and developing pods. Severe infections cause complete chlorotic yellowing, premature defoliation, and shriveled, malformed pods, inflicting yield losses exceeding forty to sixty percent. Implementing Integrated Disease Management combines timely early-sowing to escape late-season epidemic build-up, foliar spraying of wettable sulfur at two grams per liter or systemic triazoles like hexaconazole at one milliliter per liter at the first sign of lower-leaf infection, and the widespread cultivation of resistant varieties bred by the Indian Institute of Pulses Research, such as Prakash (IPFD 1-10), IPF 4-9, Aman, Gomati, HFP 529, and VL Matar-42. Concurrently, field pea rust, caused by Uromyces fabae, produces circular reddish-brown pustules that are managed through crop rotation and prophylactic mancozeb applications.

Among alternative cool-season grain legumes, faba bean (Vicia faba), known regionally as bakla, represents an underutilized nutritional and ecological powerhouse. Indigenous to the Mediterranean and Central Asia, faba bean exhibits unmatched cold tolerance, enduring winter frosts down to minus four degrees Celsius that destroy chickpea and field pea. Possessing an erect, robust, non-lodging stem that reaches one meter in height, faba bean forms exceptionally prolific root nodulation, fixing between one hundred and two hundred kilograms of atmospheric nitrogen per hectare, the highest biological nitrogen fixation rate of any cultivated annual legume. Agronomically, faba bean produces between two point five and four tonnes of high-protein grain per hectare on fertile clay loams. However, commercial food adoption was historically restricted by anti-nutritional factors, specifically vicine and convicine, pyrimidine glucosides that trigger acute hemolytic anemia, known clinically as favism, in individuals possessing genetic glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency. Modern pulse breeding programs have successfully identified low-vicine and low-convicine genetic lines, transforming faba bean into an elite protein crop for domestic culinary consumption and balanced livestock formulations.

Grasspea, botanically classified as Lathyrus sativus and known regionally as khesari or teora, represents the ultimate ecological survival pulse across the fragile rice fallows of eastern India and Bangladesh. Exhibiting peerless resistance to severe drought, extreme flooding, and heavy, compacted soil conditions, khesari is traditionally broadcast as a zero-till paira crop into standing Aman paddy muds, yielding one point zero to one point five tonnes of grain per hectare with zero cash investment. Historically, khesari consumption was marred by the presence of a potent non-protein neurotoxic amino acid, beta-N-oxalyl-L-alpha,beta-diaminopropionic acid (ODAP, also known as BOAA). Chronic, unvaried consumption of high-ODAP wild khesari exceeding thirty percent of dietary intake over two to three months causes neurolathyrism, an irreversible neurological disorder characterized by spastic paraparesis, motor neuron degeneration, and paralysis of the lower limbs. Over decades, this health hazard led to statutory bans on commercial khesari trading. However, scientific breeding by the Indian Agricultural Research Institute and the Indian Council of Agricultural Research yielded breakthrough non-toxic varieties—such as Ratan, Mahateora, and Prateek—which contain safe, benign ODAP concentrations below zero point one percent. Consequently, statutory food authorities modernized regulations, lifting prohibitions and reintegrating low-ODAP khesari into national pulse markets.

The integrated production economics of field pea establish it as one of the most lucrative and financially dependable rabi crops across northern and central India. With high-yielding cultivars yielding between two point zero and two point eight tonnes of clean grain per hectare under one or two light irrigations, gross revenues consistently range between ninety thousand and one hundred and thirty thousand rupees per hectare based on prevailing commercial market prices of four thousand five hundred to five thousand five hundred rupees per quintal. Because biological nitrogen fixation curtails synthetic fertilizer costs, paid-out cultivation expenses rarely exceed twenty thousand to twenty-five thousand rupees per hectare, delivering net operating profits of seventy thousand to one hundred and five thousand rupees per hectare across a four-month winter window. Furthermore, field pea generates two to three tonnes of soft, nutrient-rich stover (matar bhusa) that commands premium prices among dairy cattle owners. By pairing high-yielding powdery mildew resistant varieties with disciplined disease surveillance and sound residual moisture management, cool-season grain legumes deliver extraordinary agronomic resilience, profound soil regeneration, and lucrative farm profits.
