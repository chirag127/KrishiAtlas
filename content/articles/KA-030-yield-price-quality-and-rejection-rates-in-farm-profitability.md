# Yield, Price, Quality and Rejection Rates in Farm Profitability

In agricultural financial modeling, revenue projections are frequently constructed around an idealized equation: total physical biological yield multiplied by an assumed top-tier wholesale market price. In real-world commercial farming, this naive formulation is profoundly misleading. Agricultural commodities are never homogeneous, markets do not absorb all harvested volumes indiscriminately, and wholesale buyers do not pay uniform prices across an entire harvest. Realized farm profitability is governed by the marketable yield equation, which accounts for the interplay between biological volume, quality grading differentials, sorting rejection percentages, and statutory market deductions. Understanding these commercial mechanics is essential for preventing revenue shortfalls.

## The Marketable Yield Equation

To capture the financial reality of agricultural marketing, economists define net realized revenue through the marketable yield equation. 

Net realized enterprise revenue is calculated as total gross biological harvest volume, multiplied by the marketable recovery percentage after deducting post-harvest sorting losses, multiplied by the volume-weighted average price realized across distinct quality grades, minus statutory market deductions, commission fees, and packaging freight.

This equation reveals why focusing exclusively on maximizing gross physical yield can lead to financial disappointment. A producer who increases physical tomato yield from fifteen tonnes to twenty tonnes per acre through aggressive nitrogen application and heavy irrigation may actually reduce net profit if the resulting fruits are watery, soft-skinned, and highly prone to cracking. 

If five tonnes of that harvest are rejected at the sorting shed due to skin splits and fungal decay, and the remaining fifteen tonnes command lower wholesale prices due to poor shelf life, the cultivator incurs higher harvesting, crate handling, and transport costs for an enterprise that delivers lower net cash revenue than a well-managed fifteen-tonne harvest of firm, premium-grade produce.

## Quality Grading and Price Stratification

In wholesale agricultural trade, produce is systematically categorized into commercial quality grades, each commanding dramatically divergent price realizations.

In commercial horticulture—encompassing fruits and vegetables such as tomatoes, bell peppers, apples, onions, and table potatoes—harvested produce is separated during post-harvest grading into Grade A, Grade B, Grade C, and un-marketable culls. 

Grade A produce represents the commercial pinnacle: uniform physical dimensions, optimal varietal coloration, firm flesh, zero skin blemishes, and absence of mechanical or pest damage. Grade A produce is absorbed by modern organized retail chains, institutional supermarket buyers, and high-end urban wholesale markets, commanding top-tier auction prices.

Grade B produce exhibits minor aesthetic imperfections, such as slight skin discoloration, slight asymmetrical curvature, or minor superficial scars that do not impair nutritional value or immediate edibility. In wholesale Agricultural Produce Market Committee mandis, Grade B produce routinely trades at thirty to forty percent discounts below Grade A benchmarks, absorbed by traditional neighborhood street vendors and informal suburban markets.

Grade C produce comprises undersized, oversized, or visibly misshapen specimens. It clears wholesale auctions only during periods of general market scarcity, suffering steep price discounts of sixty to seventy-five percent, frequently purchased by commercial food processors or mass institutional mess kitchens at prices barely covering transport freight. 

Finally, un-marketable culls—consisting of pest-bored, rot-infected, bruised, or severely cracked produce—possess zero commercial value. Culls not only generate zero revenue, but also impose explicit cash disposal costs, requiring labour to segregate, haul away, and bury to prevent disease spread across the farm.

## Post-Harvest Rejection Rates and Sorting Losses

A critical parameter often underestimated in agricultural business planning is the post-harvest sorting rejection rate.

In perishable crop cultivation, sorting rejection rates across Indian agricultural supply chains routinely consume fifteen to thirty percent of gross harvest volume. Rejection occurs across multiple stages of the handling chain. In the field, manual harvest labourers harvesting in extreme afternoon heat or packing un-cushioned wooden crates cause mechanical bruising that triggers rapid tissue breakdown. In the packing shed, sorting removes produce failing buyer size, maturity, and firmness specifications.

During transport, inadequate shock absorption on unpaved rural roads, combined with high ambient temperatures in un-refrigerated trucks, induces transit spoilage. If an agribusiness venture budgets for an annual output of one hundred tonnes of high-value capsicum, but experiences an average post-harvest rejection rate of twenty percent between the field and the wholesale receiving dock, the effective revenue-generating output collapses to eighty tonnes. 

Because all pre-harvest input expenditures—hybrid seeds, drip irrigation, specialized foliar nutrition, and labour—were expended across the entire one hundred tonnes, the effective unit cost of production per saleable kilogram escalates by twenty-five percent. Failing to budget for realistic rejection rates transforms seemingly profitable business models into structural loss-makers.

## Moisture Content and Quality Standards in Foodgrain Procurement

Quality-based price deductions are not confined to perishable horticulture; they govern statutory price realization in broadacre cereal and pulse procurement.

Under the uniform quality specifications established by the Department of Food and Public Distribution and executed by the Food Corporation of India for Minimum Support Price procurement of paddy and wheat, strict statutory thresholds are enforced. For paddy, the maximum permissible moisture content at the procurement center is fixed at seventeen percent. 

If a farmer delivers freshly harvested paddy containing twenty percent moisture due to late rains or early morning harvesting, government procurement agencies either reject the consignment outright or enforce statutory moisture cuts, known as nami katauti. For every percentage point of moisture exceeding the seventeen percent ceiling, the procurement officer deducts a proportional weight allowance from the farmer's delivery receipt, reducing net cash payment.

Furthermore, grain consignments are subjected to laboratory grain analysis measuring foreign organic matter, inorganic dust, damaged and discolored grains, immature shriveled grains, and weevil-infested kernels. Consignments exceeding statutory tolerance limits face price penalties or require the farmer to pay private mandi labourers to manually winnow, sieve, and dry the grain on open concrete mandi yards, incurring substantial cash deductions before the harvest is accepted.

## Mandi Deductions and Market Friction

The final erosion of realized farm revenue occurs through institutional and customary deductions enforced at wholesale Agricultural Produce Market Committee mandis.

When a farmer offloads produce at an APMC market yard, the gross auction price agreed upon by the commission agent, known as the arhtiya, does not equal the net cash disbursed to the farmer. In many regional mandis, institutional practices impose layered deductions. These include statutory market fees ranging from one to two percent, commission agent fees traditionally deducted from the seller or buyer, unloading and handling fees paid to market porters known as palledari, and weighing charges.

More critically, informal customary practices often impose hidden deductions. In fruit and vegetable mandis, buyers frequently enforce unrecorded weight deductions, known historically as dhalta or chhut, under the pretext of accounting for container weight, dirt adhesion, or expected transit shrinkage. 

In addition, commission agents frequently enforce arbitrary quality deductions on produce auctioned under opaque open-cover bidding, known as hathha, claiming that lower layers of produce inside sacks or crates were inferior to top display samples. For commercial farmers, navigating these market realities requires establishing strict on-farm grading, investing in field pre-cooling, and cultivating relationships with verified direct institutional off-takers to capture the true economic value of their agricultural harvest.
