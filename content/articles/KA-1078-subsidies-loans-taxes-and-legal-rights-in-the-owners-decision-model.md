# Subsidies, Loans, Taxes and Legal Rights in the Owner’s Decision Model

Navigating the statutory interface between corporate employment, land ownership, institutional credit, government subsidy programs, and income tax regulations is critical when evaluating an agricultural transition. Naive agricultural plans frequently treat government subsidies and concessional bank loans as effortless cash windfalls. In practice, accessing agricultural policy incentives requires meeting strict statutory eligibility criteria linked to land title, computerized revenue records, and tax filings. For an early-career software engineer contemplating redeployment of capital into family land held in their father's name, understanding these institutional mechanisms is essential.

## The Statutory Title Barrier to Subsidies and Credit

The most significant structural constraint confronting an adult child seeking to develop family land is the legal dichotomy between personal financing and recorded ownership. 

In India, all central and state agricultural support mechanisms, including institutional credit facilities, direct benefit transfer subsidies, and disaster relief compensation, are tied strictly to the registered land title holder documented in the official state revenue record, known as the Khatauni. Under the Uttar Pradesh Revenue Code of 2006, if the family land in Ghaziabad is recorded exclusively in the father's name, the father is the sole legal entity recognized by government departments, public sector banks, and agricultural cooperatives.

The owner possesses zero independent legal standing to execute a mortgage, apply for a subsidized bank loan, or register on government subsidy portals in their personal name. If the owner invests personal corporate salary savings into drilling a solar tubewell or constructing a shade net nursery, all formal applications for government capital subsidies must be submitted in the father's name, linked to the father's Aadhaar card and bank account. 

Unless the father executes a registered Gift Deed under the Transfer of Property Act or undergoes a formal revenue mutation adding the son as a joint Bhumidhar with Transferable Rights, the owner cannot build an independent agricultural credit history or assert direct legal ownership over state-subsidized farm assets.

## Institutional Agricultural Credit: The Kisan Credit Card Framework

Institutional agricultural lending in India provides deeply subsidized working capital through the Kisan Credit Card scheme, administered by the Reserve Bank of India and the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development.

For short-term crop cultivation, the Kisan Credit Card provides credit limits pegged to the scale of finance established by the District Level Technical Committee for specific crops in Ghaziabad district. The baseline interest rate is seven percent per annum. Under the central Interest Subvention Scheme, farmers who settle their crop loans promptly within one year receive a three percent Prompt Repayment Incentive, effectively reducing the net borrowing cost to an extraordinary four percent per annum for credit limits up to three lakh rupees.

In addition, under Reserve Bank of India guidelines, agricultural loans up to one lakh sixty thousand rupees are completely collateral-free, requiring no hypothecation of land title deeds. For higher limits up to three lakh rupees, banks register a simple equitable mortgage or charge on the land through the online revenue portal without expensive stamp duty. 

However, utilizing Kisan Credit Card debt requires strict financial discipline. If crop revenues fail or cash proceeds are diverted to non-farm consumption, unpaid crop loans quickly lose the three percent subvention, causing interest rates to revert to commercial rates exceeding twelve percent and triggering default notices that impair family creditworthiness.

## Major Central and Uttar Pradesh State Agricultural Subsidies

Significant government capital subsidy schemes are available to modernize peri-urban landholdings, provided applicants navigate state administrative portals successfully.

Under Component B and Component C of the Pradhan Mantri Kisan Urja Suraksha evam Utthaan Mahabhiyan, known as PM-KUSUM, farmers in Uttar Pradesh can replace diesel tubewells with standalone solar irrigation pumps or solarize existing grid-connected tubewells. The central government provides thirty percent capital subsidy, matched by an additional thirty percent subsidy from the government of Uttar Pradesh, leaving the landholder to fund only forty percent through personal capital or concessional bank debt.

Under the Sub-Mission on Agricultural Mechanization administered through the Uttar Pradesh Agriculture Department portal, individual farmers and rural youth can secure forty to fifty percent subsidies on tractor-drawn agricultural machinery, including rotavators, laser land levelers, and super seeders. For entrepreneurs establishing a Custom Hiring Center, capital assistance of forty percent is available on project investments up to ten lakh rupees.

Similarly, under the Mission for Integrated Development of Horticulture, managed by the Uttar Pradesh Horticulture and Food Processing Department, capital subsidies of fifty percent are provided for erecting commercial shade net houses, naturally ventilated polyhouses, and establishing small-scale plant nurseries. However, navigating these schemes requires dealing with physical field inspections by the District Horticulture Officer, online quota lotteries, and substantial administrative processing delays before subsidy disbursements are credited.

## Income Tax Architecture: Section 10(1) and the Partial Integration Rule

The taxation of agricultural income in India represents one of the most misunderstood aspects of personal financial planning. While agricultural income is broadly described as tax-free, its interaction with corporate salary income is governed by complex statutory provisions.

Under Section 10(1) of the Indian Income-tax Act of 1961, agricultural income is entirely exempt from central income tax. Section 2(1A) defines agricultural income strictly as rent or revenue derived from land situated in India used for agricultural purposes, income derived from actual agricultural cultivation, and income from basic primary processing operations necessary to render raw produce marketable. Consequently, cash rental income received from leasing family land out under a registered theka agreement or profits generated from direct field cultivation qualify fully as exempt agricultural income under Section 10(1).

However, to prevent high-income individuals from exploiting this exemption, the Indian tax code enforces the Partial Integration Method. Under this rule, if an individual's non-agricultural income exceeds the basic tax exemption threshold and their net agricultural income exceeds five thousand rupees, the agricultural income is added to non-agricultural income solely to determine the applicable marginal tax slab rate applied to the non-agricultural earnings.

In the case of the owner, who earns an entry-level corporate salary of three lakh sixty thousand rupees at Tata Consultancy Services, this rule produces a remarkably favorable outcome. Under the default New Tax Regime under Section 115BAC, the standard deduction of seventy-five thousand rupees reduces taxable corporate salary to two lakh eighty-five thousand rupees. Because this non-agricultural income is strictly below the basic exemption limit of three lakh rupees, the partial integration formula does not apply. The owner's corporate salary incurs zero central income tax, and all family agricultural cash lease income or farm profits remain one hundred percent tax-free, maximizing household capital accumulation.
