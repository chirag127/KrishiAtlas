Sweet potato, botanically classified as Ipomoea batatas within the Convolvulaceae morning-glory family, represents one of the most resilient, nutritionally dense, and calorically productive tropical tuber crops in global agriculture. Distinct from the Irish solanaceous potato and monocotyledonous dioscorean yams, sweet potato is a dicotyledonous perennial vine cultivated agronomically as a rapid-cycle annual, delivering harvestable storage roots within one hundred to one hundred twenty days of planting. In India, sweet potato occupies approximately one hundred thirty thousand hectares, generating an annual harvest exceeding one point one million tonnes, with intensive production concentrated across eastern and southern states including Odisha, West Bengal, Kerala, Bihar, and Uttar Pradesh. Historically categorized as a famine-reserve survival food of marginal tribal communities, sweet potato is undergoing an extraordinary commercial resurgence driven by the development of biofortified, orange-fleshed cultivars that tackle pervasive micronutrient deficiencies, alongside expanding industrial applications in functional flours, alcohol distillation, and natural food colorants.

The genetic landscape of sweet potato has been revolutionized by targeted breeding programs led by the ICAR Central Tuber Crops Research Institute and the International Potato Center. Traditional Indian landraces produced white or cream-fleshed storage roots characterized by high starch content exceeding twenty-five percent and dry matter approaching thirty percent, but containing virtually zero provitamin A carotenoids. Modern biofortified cultivars, preeminently Bhu Sona and Bhu Kanti, have completely redefined nutritional quality. Bhu Sona produces elliptic storage roots featuring deep-orange flesh enriched with fourteen milligrams of beta-carotene per one hundred grams of fresh root weight, equivalent to more than twenty thousand international units of vitamin A activity, easily fulfilling the daily recommended nutritional allowance of young children and nursing mothers with a single modest portion. Concurrently, Bhu Kanti delivers ten point eight milligrams of beta-carotene per hundred grams while exhibiting superior tolerance to mid-season drought and salinity levels up to six to eight deciSiemens per meter. In commercial fields, these modern biofortified varieties yield twenty to twenty-four tonnes of marketable storage roots per hectare, combining nutritional density with commercial profitability.

Establishing a high-yielding sweet potato crop requires precise vegetative propagation protocols. Unlike true seed or tuber pieces, sweet potato is propagated vegetatively via terminal vine cuttings. The physiological quality of the planting material dictates early crop establishment and adventitious root initiation. Commercial growers maintain a two-stage nursery system: primary nurseries are established by planting healthy, sprouted mother storage roots into fertile raised beds; when emerging vines reach thirty to forty centimeters, they are harvested and transferred to secondary multiplication nurseries to generate abundant, uniform planting material. For main field planting, terminal vine cuttings measuring twenty to thirty centimeters in length, possessing three to four active vegetative nodes, are excised from healthy nursery stands. Research consistently demonstrates that terminal cuttings yield fifteen to twenty-five percent higher storage root tonnage than middle or basal vine sections, as terminal apical meristems possess higher endogenous auxin concentrations and lower viral loads. Cuttings should be shaded for one to two days prior to planting to stimulate adventitious root primordia emergence.

Land preparation and field geometry govern the physical mechanics of root expansion. Sweet potato demands well-drained, loose, sandy loam or alluvial soils having a neutral to slightly acidic pH between five point eight and six point eight. Heavy, poorly drained vertisols induce waterlogging, causing root lenticels to rupture, predisposing roots to bacterial rots, and producing misshapen, cracked storage roots. Commercial planting is executed on raised ridges spaced ninety centimeters apart with a ridge height of forty to forty-five centimeters, or on rounded mounds spaced seventy-five centimeters apart. Terminal vine cuttings are inserted into the ridge crest at intra-row spacings of twenty to twenty-five centimeters, burying the central two nodes horizontally three to four centimeters beneath the soil surface while leaving terminal leaves exposed. Planting on high raised ridges ensures loose, uncompacted soil zones that offer minimal physical resistance to swelling roots, while facilitating rapid gravitational drainage during heavy monsoon cloudbursts.

Nutrient management in sweet potato demands a balanced hormonal approach. Excessive application of chemical nitrogen stimulates aggressive, rampant foliar vine growth at the expense of storage root swelling, causing a physiological condition where the crop produces an impenetrable green foliar blanket yielding only thin, fibrous, unmarketable pencil roots. ICAR recommendations prescribe an integrated basal dressing of five tonnes of well-decomposed farmyard manure per hectare combined with seventy-five kilograms of nitrogen, twenty-five kilograms of phosphorus, and seventy-five kilograms of potassium per hectare. Nitrogen is strictly split, applying half at planting and the remaining half thirty to forty days later during manual earthing-up. High potassium nutrition is indispensable, as potassium activates starch synthases and drives the phloem translocation of photosynthates from foliar canopies into subterranean root storage parenchyma.

The primary entomological adversary threatening commercial sweet potato cultivation across India is the sweet potato weevil, scientifically classified as Cylas formicarius. This small, metallic-blue and reddish-orange curculionid beetle causes severe yield destruction, regularly destroying thirty to sixty percent of crop value in unprotected fields. Adult females excavate small cavities in exposed storage roots or lower vine collars, depositing single eggs that hatch into legless white grubs. Grubs bore tortuous tunnels through the storage parenchyma, fouling the internal tissue with dark frass and secondary bacterial rots. In response to weevil feeding and mechanical wounding, the storage root synthesizes toxic, highly bitter sesquiterpenoid phytoalexins, predominantly ipomeamarone and ipomeamaronol. The accumulation of these terpenoid compounds renders the entire root completely inedible, bitter, and hepatotoxic, leading to total rejection by fresh culinary consumers and livestock alike.

Managing sweet potato weevil requires an integrated cultural and semiochemical strategy. Because adult weevils are weak fliers that access roots primarily through surface soil cracks caused by drying soil, farmers must perform two thorough earthing-up operations at thirty and forty-five days after planting to keep swelling roots continuously buried under five to eight centimeters of loose soil. Crop sanitation is vital, ensuring all crop residues and volunteer vines are removed and destroyed immediately post-harvest. Pheromone mass trapping deploying synthetic female sex pheromones, commercially formulated as Cylas-lure, suspended in plastic funnel traps at ten to fifteen units per hectare, captures thousands of searching male beetles, suppressing localized reproductive cycles. Furthermore, harvesting roots promptly at physiological maturity prevents late-season weevil population build-up in drying soils.

Post-harvest handling and curing determine the storage longevity and culinary grade of the harvest. Immediately upon harvest, sweet potato roots are acutely vulnerable to skinning, bruising, and fungal infection through mechanical abrasions. Freshly dug roots must undergo an on-farm curing protocol, held in warm, humid conditions at twenty-eight to thirty degrees Celsius with eighty-five to ninety percent relative humidity for four to seven days. Curing triggers the rapid physiological suberization of wounded epidermal layers, forming a thick, protective corky wound periderm that seals cuts against opportunistic pathogens like Rhizopus soft rot. Concurrently, curing stimulates internal beta-amylase enzymes that partially hydrolyze insoluble starch into sweet maltose sugars, enhancing culinary sweetness and palate appeal. Cured roots stored in ventilated, darkened, cool thatch godowns maintain commercial freshness for three to four months. By pairing biofortified orange-fleshed varieties with terminal vine selection, raised-ridge soil architecture, disciplined weevil IPM, and proper wound curing, commercial producers establish sweet potato as a highly remunerative, climate-resilient horticultural enterprise.
