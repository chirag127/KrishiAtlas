# The Green Revolution: Gains, Costs and Regional Differences

The Green Revolution of the mid-1960s represents the most dramatic technological and institutional transformation in modern agricultural history. In response to recurring famines, severe back-to-back monsoon droughts, and humiliating geopolitical vulnerability, India deployed an intensive technological package that fundamentally altered its food production trajectory. Within a single decade, the nation shattered the Malthusian trap, transitioned from chronic food deficits to national grain self-sufficiency, and established sovereign food reserves. However, this production miracle was achieved through severe ecological compromises, groundwater depletion, and acute regional economic disparities that shape agricultural policy to this day.

## The Geopolitical Crisis and the Drive for Food Sovereignty

In the mid-1960s, India confronted a severe existential food crisis. Severe droughts in 1965 and 1966 devastated agricultural output, reducing national foodgrain production to barely seventy-two million tonnes and threatening millions of citizens with widespread famine. 

The Indian government was forced to rely on massive grain imports from the United States under Public Law 480 Title One, importing over ten million tonnes of American wheat in 1966 alone. This precarious food pipeline was derisively characterized as a ship-to-mouth existence, wherein international grain shipments arrived barely in time to avert urban starvation. When foreign food shipments were leveraged politically to influence Indian foreign policy, national leadership, spearheaded by Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri, Union Agriculture Minister C. Subramaniam, and visionary scientists Dr. M. S. Swaminathan and Dr. Norman Borlaug, resolved to achieve uncompromising food sovereignty through science-based agricultural intensification.

## The Technological Architecture: Seeds, Water and Chemicals

The core biological breakthrough of the Green Revolution was the development and rapid dissemination of semi-dwarf, high-yielding crop varieties. 

Traditional tall cereal varieties possessed thin, elongated stalks. When farmers applied heavy doses of chemical fertilizers and irrigation water, the plants grew excessively tall and top-heavy; the heavy grain heads caused the weak stems to buckle and collapse into the mud before harvest, a biological failure known as lodging. Plant breeders resolved this genetic constraint by incorporating dwarfing genes. In wheat, breeders at the International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center in Mexico utilized the Japanese Norin-Ten dwarfing gene to develop stiff-strawed dwarf lines. Introduced to India, these lines were adapted into high-yielding, rust-resistant cultivars such as Kalyansona and Sonalika. In rice, the International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines utilized the Taiwanese Dee-geo-woo-gen dwarfing gene to develop the legendary IR-8 cultivar, subsequently adapted in India as Jaya and Padma.

These semi-dwarf varieties fundamentally restructured plant physiology. Instead of allocating photosynthates into vegetative stems and leaves, dwarf plants channeled energy directly into the grain head, elevating the harvest index to over fifty percent. Furthermore, the stiff, compact stalks did not lodge when treated with high chemical fertility, allowing crops to absorb unprecedented quantities of synthetic nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.

However, high-yielding varieties were not miracle seeds in isolation; they functioned only as part of an integrated technological package. They demanded controlled, timely, and frequent water applications, catalyzing a rapid expansion of private electric and diesel tubewells. They required heavy applications of chemical fertilizers, particularly subsidized urea and diammonium phosphate, alongside prophylactic chemical insecticides and synthetic herbicides to control weeds that thrived in fertilized fields.

## Production Triumphs and Buffer Stock Security

The agricultural response to this technological package was unprecedented in speed and magnitude. India's national wheat production, which stood at twelve point three million tonnes in 1964-65, jumped to over twenty million tonnes by 1970-71, representing an extraordinary sixty-five percent increase in six years. 

Total national foodgrain production expanded rapidly from seventy-two million tonnes in the mid-1960s to over one hundred and thirty million tonnes by the early 1980s, eventually surpassing three hundred and thirty million tonnes in recent harvest records. India decisively eliminated catastrophic famine, ended its dependence on foreign food concessions, and built vast sovereign buffer stocks of wheat and rice. Managed by the Food Corporation of India, these reserves underpinned the Public Distribution System, stabilized urban market prices, and provided the legal food entitlements codified under the National Food Security Act of 2013.

## Regional Disparities and the Cereal Bias

Despite its national success, the Green Revolution suffered from deep structural inequities in geographic and crop distribution.

The rollout of the high-yielding technology was deliberately concentrated in regions possessing pre-existing irrigation infrastructure, consolidated landholdings, and paved rural roads: specifically Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh, and selected canal deltas in Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. These progressive agricultural tracts absorbed the overwhelming majority of public tubewell electrification subsidies, rural credit disbursements, and price-support procurement infrastructure. 

In contrast, the vast agricultural belts of Eastern India, including Bihar, West Bengal, and Odisha, alongside the rainfed drylands of Central and Peninsular India, were largely bypassed for three decades. While northwestern farmers accumulated wealth, modernized machinery, and expanded farm assets, millions of smallholders in rainfed zones remained trapped in subsistence farming, severely widening inter-regional economic inequality.

Furthermore, public agricultural policy developed an acute cereal bias. Government price supports, minimum support prices, and open-ended grain procurement focused almost exclusively on wheat and paddy. Traditional, climate-resilient pulses like chickpea, pigeon pea, and lentil, alongside nutrient-dense millets such as sorghum, pearl millet, and finger millet, were systematically marginalized. Cultivators diverted fertile, irrigated acreage away from indigenous pulses and oilseeds into rice and wheat monocultures, eroding national dietary diversity and generating chronic domestic shortfalls in pulses and edible oils that persist today.

## Ecological Degradation and Long-Term Sustainability Costs

Over sixty years of continuous, intensive cereal monoculture have extracted an immense ecological and agronomic toll across the original Green Revolution heartlands.

The most catastrophic environmental externality is the collapse of groundwater aquifers. To sustain water-intensive paddy cultivation across semi-arid northwestern plains during the peak evaporation heat of May and June, millions of farmers drilled deep tubewells powered by unmetered, subsidized electricity. According to statutory assessments by the Central Ground Water Board, over seventy-five percent of administrative blocks in Punjab and Haryana are classified as over-exploited, with water tables dropping by fifty centimeters to one meter annually. Shallow aquifers have been completely exhausted, forcing farmers into debt to drill deeper centrifugal bores and install expensive multi-stage submersible pumps tapping ancient, non-renewable aquifers.

Soil health has suffered parallel degradation. Decades of heavy synthetic urea application, distorted by price subsidies that made nitrogen extraordinarily cheap relative to potassium and phosphorus, shattered soil chemistry. The continuous flooding of paddy fields followed by intensive wheat tillage broke down soil organic carbon, which plunged below zero point three percent across vast tracts of northwestern India. Depleted of organic matter, soils developed severe multi-micronutrient deficiencies in zinc, iron, sulfur, and boron, requiring ever-increasing chemical applications simply to maintain stagnant baseline yields.

Public health and atmospheric externalities have compounded this crisis. Decades of un-calibrated broad-spectrum pesticide spraying contaminated rural drinking water aquifers, creating acute toxicological burdens associated with elevated cancer rates and endocrine disorders in farming communities. Furthermore, the mechanization of paddy harvesting with heavy combine harvesters, coupled with a tight fifteen-day window to sow winter wheat, gave rise to widespread open-field burning of millions of tonnes of rice straw. This seasonal burning unleashes dense toxic smog across the entire Indo-Gangetic Plain and the National Capital Region every autumn, imposing severe health and economic penalties on over one hundred million citizens. The contemporary challenge of Indian agriculture is therefore not merely producing more grain, but executing a comprehensive ecological transition toward regenerative, water-secure, and diversified farming systems.
