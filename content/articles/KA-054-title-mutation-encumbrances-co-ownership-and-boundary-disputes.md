# Title, Mutation, Encumbrances, Co-Ownership and Boundary Disputes

Property law in agricultural India operates across two parallel, frequently contradictory administrative tracks: the civil registration of deeds and the administrative maintenance of revenue records. Uninformed land purchasers, inherited landholders, and prospective agribusiness operators routinely fall victim to catastrophic title defects because they conflate administrative tax records with legal ownership title. A farmer may possess an official revenue record listing their name as the sole registered cultivator, yet hold zero legally enforceable title due to prior unrecorded family partitions, pending bank liens, or fraudulent transfers. Ensuring absolute security of tenure requires mastering the legal mechanics of title deeds, understanding the statutory limitations of revenue mutations, executing comprehensive thirty-year encumbrance audits, resolving unpartitioned co-ownership risks, and enforcing statutory boundary demarcations.

## The Landmark Jurisprudence: Mutation Does Not Confer Title

The most dangerous misconception in Indian agricultural real estate is believing that an entry in the revenue record—locally termed Khatauni, Jamabandi, or Record of Rights—constitutes conclusive proof of ownership. For decades, the Supreme Court of India has consistently reiterated the foundational property law doctrine that mutation entries in revenue ledgers do not create, confer, extinguish, or transfer legal title to immovable property. Reaffirmed definitively in the landmark ruling of Jitendra Singh versus State of Madhya Pradesh in 2021, the apex court established that revenue mutation entries are maintained strictly for fiscal purposes, functioning exclusively as an administrative mechanism for the state to collect land revenue and agricultural taxes.

A revenue officer, such as a Tehsildar or Naib Tehsildar conducting summary mutation proceedings under state revenue codes, possesses zero judicial authority to declare or adjudicate substantive legal title. When a mutation is sought on the basis of an unregistered family settlement, a disputed testamentary will, or a contested verbal gift, the revenue officer cannot evaluate the evidentiary validity of the underlying transfer. If title is challenged, the claimant must institute a formal title suit before a competent civil court. Relying upon a clean Khatauni extract without examining the registered instruments of transfer in the chain of title exposes an agricultural enterprise to sudden eviction when excluded legal heirs challenge the transaction in civil court.

## The Thirty-Year Encumbrance Audit and Chain of Title Verification

Establishing unencumbered ownership title requires conducting a rigorous thirty-year title search at the jurisdictional Sub-Registrar Office where deeds of conveyance are formally registered under the Registration Act of 1908. Real estate jurisprudence dictates a thirty-year retrospective search because the Indian Limitation Act prescribes a twelve-year limitation period for recovering possession of immovable property based on title, while claims involving mortgaged properties, minors asserting inheritance rights upon attaining majority, or government easements frequently extend across multiple decades.

The core evidentiary instrument of this audit is the Encumbrance Certificate, issued by the Sub-Registrar. The Encumbrance Certificate records every registered transaction affecting the specific Khasra or survey number across the requested time horizon, detailing previous registered sale deeds, gift deeds, mortgage liens created in favor of commercial lending institutions, court attachments, and long-term registered lease agreements. Crucially, an encumbrance search must trace an unbroken, sequential chain of title transfers from the original ancestral holder down to the prospective seller. If a link in the chain is missing—such as an unrecorded inheritance where title passed verbally without formal succession documentation or registered release deeds from surviving sisters—the title remains legally clouded and vulnerable to partition suits.

## The Perils of Unpartitioned Co-Ownership and Coparcenary Estates

Agricultural holdings across India are predominantly held as undivided co-ownerships or Hindu Undivided Family coparcenary estates. When an ancestral landowner dies intestate, their legal heirs—encompassing sons, daughters, and surviving spouses under the Hindu Succession Act of 1956 as amended in 2005—automatically become joint co-owners. In an unpartitioned holding, each co-sharer owns an undivided proportional fraction of the entire property; no single co-owner owns a specific, physically demarcated parcel or boundary corner. Every co-owner holds a proprietary legal interest in every single square inch of the agricultural land.

This unpartitioned state creates an acute legal trap for prospective purchasers and long-term farm lessees. A single co-owner will frequently attempt to sell or lease a specific, highly desirable roadside corner of the ancestral farm, executing a deed that purports to transfer physical possession of that exact plot. Under Section Forty-Four of the Transfer of Property Act of 1882, a purchaser of an undivided share steps merely into the shoes of the transferring co-owner, acquiring a right to enforce partition, but acquiring zero legal right to exclusive physical possession of that specific corner. If the remaining co-owners object, they can file an injunction suit, immediately halting all farming operations and restraining the purchaser from entering the land until a protracted partition suit is formally decreed by a revenue court, a judicial process that frequently consumes eight to fifteen years. Transacting with co-owned agricultural land requires that all living legal co-sharers execute the transfer deed simultaneously, or that a formal partition deed is legally executed, registered, and physically mutated across revenue maps prior to entering the transaction.

## Boundary Demarcation and Revenue Code Dispute Adjudication

Physical boundary ambiguity is an endemic generator of violent rural disputes and operational paralysis. Over generations, physical field boundaries demarcated by earthen medhs gradually shift as aggressive neighbors systematically encroach onto adjoining furrows during seasonal ploughing. When a new agricultural operator attempts to erect permanent chain-link fencing or excavate irrigation trenches along historical boundaries, neighbors frequently resist with physical force, claiming customary possession.

Resolving boundary conflicts requires invoking formal statutory demarcation mechanisms under state revenue legislation, such as Section Twenty-Four of the Uttar Pradesh Revenue Code of 2006. An operator must not engage in private physical confrontations or erect disputed fences unilaterally. Instead, a formal boundary demarcation application, locally known as hudbandi or paimaish, must be filed before the Sub-Divisional Officer or Tehsildar, accompanied by statutory measurement fees. The revenue authority appoints a field revenue team, comprising the revenue Inspector and local Lekhpal, who issue formal statutory notices to all adjoining landowners establishing a fixed survey date. Utilizing calibrated measuring chains, total stations, and official traced village Shajra maps, the revenue team establishes permanent fixed boundary coordinates anchored to historical stone triangulation pillars, driving official boundary markers into the soil. Once the Sub-Divisional Officer confirms the demarcation report and issues an official order, any subsequent tampering with boundary pillars constitutes a punishable criminal offense under the Indian Penal Code, providing the agricultural operator with lawful, police-enforced security of physical possession.
