# Chickpea: Sowing, Nodulation and Terminal Heat Risks

Chickpea, designated botanically as Cicer arietinum and recognized across the Indian subcontinent as chana or Bengal gram, represents the premier pulse crop of South Asia, accounting for nearly fifty percent of total domestic pulse production and anchoring the protein security of vegetarian diets. Dominated by two distinct commercial and morphological types—the angular, pigmented, small-seeded desi type and the smooth, cream-colored, large-seeded kabuli type—chickpea is cultivated primarily as a post-monsoon winter rabi crop across central, northern, and peninsular India. While prized for its profound capacity to enrich soil fertility through symbiotic biological nitrogen fixation, chickpea agronomy represents a delicate balancing act against environmental stress. Successful production requires precise calibration of sowing windows, deep seed placement in receding moisture profiles, active management of root nodulation, and aggressive physiological mitigation against terminal heat stress that increasingly curtails reproductive grain filling under shifting climate patterns.

The temporal management of chickpea sowing is strictly bounded by thermal dynamics and residual soil moisture availability. Sowing too early in late September or early October, when ambient day temperatures still exceed thirty-two degrees Celsius, triggers severe seedling mortality, excessive vegetative etiolation, and heightened susceptibility to soil-borne collar rot caused by Sclerotium rolfsii and dry root rot caused by Rhizoctonia bataticola. Conversely, delaying sowing beyond mid-November in northern and central plains compresses the reproductive phenology of the crop, forcing flowering and pod development directly into the scorching heatwaves of late February and March. Under rainfed dryland conditions on deep black Vertisols across Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Karnataka, the optimum sowing window falls precisely between the second week of October and the first week of November, capitalizing on receding subsoil moisture before surface layers completely desiccate. In irrigated northern tracts following kharif rice or cotton, timely sowing extends from late October to the fifteenth of November. Sowing depth must be adjusted to soil hydrology: while shallow sowing at three to four centimeters is standard in irrigated fine seedbeds, rainfed chickpea must be drilled deep into the soil profile at eight to ten centimeters, placing the seed firmly within the moist capillary zone beneath the dry surface crust to ensure rapid, uniform radicle emergence.

Seedbed preparation and physical soil requirements reflect chickpea's acute vulnerability to soil compaction and temporary waterlogging. The crop demands a rough, friable, well-aerated seedbed prepared through one deep summer plowing to shatter hard subsoil pans, followed by two passes with a spring-tine cultivator and light planking. Excessive pulverization that creates a powdery fine dust must be avoided, as fine dust forms a dense surface crust following winter showers that impedes gaseous exchange. Because chickpea root systems possess virtually zero internal aerenchyma tissue, standing surface water or saturated soil conditions lasting longer than twenty-four hours induce severe hypoxic asphyxiation in the rhizosphere. Root respiration halts, toxic ethanol and lactic acid accumulate within cortical cells, and the plant experiences sudden wilting and leaf drop. Consequently, cultivation must be restricted to well-drained loams, sandy clay loams, and Vertisols with neutral to slightly alkaline reaction ranging between potential of hydrogen 6.5 and 8.5, avoiding heavy, water-trapping depressions and saline sodic soils.

Biological nitrogen fixation in chickpea operates through a highly specialized, host-specific symbiotic mutualism with the soil bacterium Mesorhizobium ciceri. Unlike promiscuous legumes that readily form nodules with diverse native soil rhizobia, chickpea requires specific Mesorhizobium strains to initiate root hair curling, infection thread formation, and indeterminate nodule organogenesis. In soils where chickpea has not been cultivated for several seasons, or in newly developed agricultural tracts, native rhizobial populations are negligible, resulting in pale, nitrogen-starved plants that fail to thrive. Mandatory on-farm bio-inoculation involves coating certified seed with approved carrier-based Mesorhizobium ciceri culture at twenty-five grams per kilogram of seed, augmented with phosphate-solubilizing bacteria such as Pseudomonas striata or Bacillus megaterium. When executing seed treatment, farmers must adhere to a strict sequence: apply chemical fungicides like carbendazim or thiram first to eradicate seed-borne pathogens, allow the seed coat to dry thoroughly in shade, and apply the live Mesorhizobium bacterial slurry last using a ten percent jaggery solution as an adhesive. Healthy, effective nodules display a deep pink to reddish interior when sliced open, signifying high concentrations of oxygen-buffering leghemoglobin, which maintains microaerophilic conditions essential for nitrogenase enzyme functionality. A well-nodulated chickpea crop fixes between sixty and one hundred and forty kilograms of atmospheric nitrogen per hectare, satisfying eighty percent of its own nitrogen requirement while leaving thirty to forty kilograms of residual nitrogen in the soil for subsequent crops.

Terminal heat stress, defined as ambient daytime temperatures exceeding thirty-two to thirty-five degrees Celsius during the flowering, pod set, and grain-filling stages, represents the most destructive environmental barrier to chickpea yield stability. Driven by rising winter temperatures and early spring heatwaves across central and peninsular India, terminal heat accelerates physiological senescence, reduces leaf chlorophyll retention, and curtails the duration of the reproductive phase by ten to twenty days. At the cellular level, thermal stress above thirty-two degrees Celsius during anthesis severely impairs pollen grain viability, inhibits pollen tube germination down the style, and induces floret abscission, resulting in sterile, empty pods. During subsequent grain filling, high temperatures denature soluble starch synthase enzymes inside the developing cotyledons, terminating starch synthesis prematurely and producing small, wrinkled, shriveled seeds that suffer severe commercial price discounts.

Mitigating terminal heat stress demands an integrated agronomic approach combining genetic evasion with physiological stress buffering. Agronomically, farmers must prioritize early-maturing, heat-tolerant varieties bred by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research, the Indian Institute of Pulses Research at Kanpur, and the International Crops Research Institute for the Semi-Arid Tropics, such as JG 14, Vijay, IPC 2011-112 (Keshav), and Pusa 362, which complete their podding cycle in ninety to one hundred and ten days before lethal March temperatures arrive. Delivering a strategic, life-saving supplemental irrigation of forty to fifty millimeters at the pod-initiation stage cools the canopy through transpirational vapor exchange and maintains cell turgor. Furthermore, foliar sprays of two percent potassium nitrate or two percent diammonium phosphate applied during early flowering and pod formation supply supplemental osmoprotectants, stabilize cellular membranes, and sustain photosynthetic carbon translocation into developing seeds, recovering fifteen to twenty-five percent of grain yield that would otherwise be lost to thermal desiccation.

The comparative production economics of desi and kabuli chickpea cultivars illustrate distinct risk-return profiles tailored to specific farm resource endowments. Desi chickpea requires lower seed investment, utilizing seventy-five to eighty kilograms of seed per hectare at standard commercial rates, exhibits superior drought hardiness, and delivers stable yields ranging from one point five to two point two tonnes per hectare under rainfed conditions. With government Minimum Support Prices fixed above five thousand four hundred rupees per quintal, desi chana generates gross grain revenues of eighty thousand to one hundred and twenty thousand rupees per hectare against paid-out cultivation costs of twenty thousand to twenty-five thousand rupees. In contrast, kabuli chickpea requires a higher seed rate of one hundred to one hundred and twenty kilograms per hectare due to bold seed size, demands assured supplemental irrigation, and exhibits extreme susceptibility to soil-borne wilt and blight. However, extra-bold kabuli varieties command premium wholesale market prices exceeding eight thousand to ten thousand rupees per quintal in urban culinary and export markets, generating gross returns surpassing one hundred and fifty thousand rupees per hectare. By matching variety duration to local thermal regimes, ensuring flawless biological seed inoculation, and executing heat-mitigation agronomy, pulse growers transform chickpea into a highly profitable, soil-regenerating winter enterprise.
