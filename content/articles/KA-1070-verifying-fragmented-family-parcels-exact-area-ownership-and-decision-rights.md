# Verifying Fragmented Family Parcels, Exact Area, Ownership and Decision Rights

In agricultural decision analysis, operating assumptions regarding land size and ownership are frequently distorted by oral traditions, colloquial land units, and informal family arrangements. For an individual contemplating capital deployment or operational responsibility, relying on oral estimates without legal verification creates substantial financial vulnerability. In the context of the Ghaziabad case study, where the property is described colloquially as nine to ten bighas and roughly five acres across several fragmented parcels, rigorous legal and cartographic due diligence is the mandatory first step before committing any financial capital.

## The Digital Record of Rights on the UP Bhulekh Portal

The state of Uttar Pradesh has digitized its rural land records through the centralized Uttar Pradesh Bhulekh online repository. The foundational legal document for any agricultural parcel is the Computerized Khatauni, which serves as the official Record of Rights under the Uttar Pradesh Revenue Code of 2006.

To verify the property, the owner must access the portal, navigate to the relevant tehsil within Ghaziabad district, such as Loni, Modinagar, or Ghaziabad Sadar, and select the specific revenue village. Searching by the father's legal name, khata number, or khasra plot number retrieves the verified current khatauni. Each parcel is assigned a unique sixteen-digit computerized land parcel identification code, which tracks all historic mutations, encumbrances, and revenue orders.

The khatauni provides definitive legal answers to three critical questions: the exact recorded area in hectares, the exact list of co-tenure holders sharing the joint khata, and whether any commercial bank has registered an active charge or lien against the land for an outstanding Kisan Credit Card loan or term debt.

## Area Conversion Mathematics: Hectares to Acres and Local Bighas

A common source of confusion in Western Uttar Pradesh land management is the divergence between metric statutory records, standard imperial units, and local customary bigha conventions. 

Official Uttar Pradesh revenue records document total parcel area, known as rakba, exclusively in hectares. One international hectare equals ten thousand square meters or approximately two point four seven one zero five standard acres. To convert the recorded hectare figure into acres, the area must be multiplied by two point four seven one.

In Western Uttar Pradesh districts including Ghaziabad, Meerut, Bulandshahr, and Hapur, the traditional land measure is the Pukka Bigha. Under standard regional revenue conventions, one Pukka Bigha equals three thousand and twenty-five square yards, twenty-seven thousand square feet, or two thousand five hundred and eight square meters. Consequently, one standard acre equals exactly one point six one three three Pukka Bighas, or conversely, one Pukka Bigha equals zero point six two acres. Each bigha is subdivided into twenty biswas, and each biswa comprises twenty biswansis.

However, certain village communities informally utilize the Kachha Bigha, which represents approximately nine thousand square feet or zero point two zero eight acres, being roughly one-third of a Pukka Bigha. If the family's oral estimate of nine to ten bighas refers to Pukka Bighas, the true physical holding comprises five point six to six point two five acres. Conversely, if the colloquial reference is to Kachha Bighas, the physical holding is merely one point eight seven to two point zero eight acres. Calculating true farm economics requires verifying the exact hectare entry on the certified khatauni rather than relying on family conversation.

## Tenure Classification Under the Uttar Pradesh Revenue Code

Agricultural land tenure in Uttar Pradesh is strictly governed by the Uttar Pradesh Revenue Code of 2006, which superseded the historic Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act of 1950. The code recognizes three primary categories of agricultural landholders: Bhumidhar with Transferable Rights, Bhumidhar with Non-Transferable Rights, and Asami.

The highest form of ownership is Bhumidhar with Transferable Rights. A landholder possessing this status holds permanent, heritable, and transferable rights in the land. They have the unrestricted right to cultivate, leave fallow, execute registered leases within statutory limits, mortgage the land to scheduled commercial banks, and bequeath the land through testamentary succession.

If the land is held as Bhumidhar with Non-Transferable Rights, the tenure holder possesses cultivation and heritable rights but cannot sell, gift, or mortgage the property. Under Section 76 of the Code, non-transferable bhumidhars generally graduate to transferable status after completing five continuous years of recorded possession, provided statutory conditions are fulfilled. The owner must confirm that the family title is recorded unequivocally as Bhumidhar with Transferable Rights.

## Joint Holdings, Coparcenary Rights and Partition Under Section 116

A major operational hurdle in Indian family agriculture is the reality of the undivided joint khata. In many ancestral holdings, the khatauni lists multiple names as joint bhumidhars, often including the father, uncles, aunts, and descendants of deceased siblings.

Under Indian property jurisprudence and the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act of 2005, daughters and sons possess equal coparcenary rights by birth in ancestral property. If an ancestral holding has not undergone formal legal partition, every recorded co-sharer owns an undivided fractional interest in every single square meter of the entire parcel. No individual co-sharer has the unilateral legal authority to earmark a specific corner of the land for specialized infrastructure, such as drilling a commercial borewell, constructing a packhouse, erecting a solar array, or planting a multi-year perennial orchard.

Under Section 116 of the Uttar Pradesh Revenue Code of 2006, any recorded co-sharer can institute a suit before the Sub-Divisional Officer for the partition of a joint holding. Partition proceedings involve preparing preliminary decrees, hearing objections, drawing up lots known as kuras by the revenue revenue inspector (kanungo), and issuing a final decree accompanied by separate computerized khatas. 

Until formal partition is completed or a legally binding registered Family Settlement Deed is executed by all adult co-sharers, sinking personal savings into permanent structures on joint family land carries severe legal risk. If family relations sour, disgruntled relatives can obtain temporary injunctions from the revenue court, halting agricultural operations and stranding invested capital.

## Cartographic Audit: Khasra, Shajra and Consolidation

Beyond textual ownership records, verifying the physical reality of the parcels requires auditing the village cadastral map, known as the Shajra, and the annual agricultural survey register, known as the Khasra.

The Khasra records field-level seasonal crop inspections conducted by the local Lekhpal, documenting which crops were sown, the irrigation source utilized, and whether any unauthorized physical possession exists on the ground. Auditing three consecutive years of khasra extracts confirms whether the parcels have been actively cultivated or abandoned to scrub vegetation.

The Shajra map provides the exact geographic geometry and location of each Khasra number. Crucially, reviewing the map reveals whether each fragmented parcel has statutory access to a public village cart track or revenue road. Under the Indian Easements Act of 1882, landlocked parcels lack independent rights of passage across neighbouring privately owned plots without formal easement agreements or designated revenue paths. 

Furthermore, historical consolidation proceedings, known as Chakbandi under the Uttar Pradesh Consolidation of Holdings Act of 1953, were designed to consolidate scattered parcels into compact agricultural blocks called chaks. If the family land remains fragmented into multiple non-contiguous parcels, it indicates that either the village was exempted from consolidation or subsequent generations fragmented the consolidated chaks through informal private sub-division. Physical demarcation by the revenue tehsildar using boundary pillars is strongly advised to prevent border disputes with aggressive adjacent landholders before initiating any new farm enterprise.
