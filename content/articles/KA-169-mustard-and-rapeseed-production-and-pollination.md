# Mustard and Rapeseed: Production and Pollination

Indian mustard and rapeseed, encompassing diverse botanically related cultivars within the genus Brassica, represent the premier rabi oilseed crops of the Indian subcontinent, driving national vegetable oil production and anchoring the rural economy across northern and central states. Dominated by Indian mustard (Brassica juncea, an amphidiploid derived from hybridization between Brassica rapa and Brassica nigra), the rapeseed-mustard complex also encompasses toria, yellow sarson, brown sarson, and gobhi sarson. Cultivated across an expansive agro-ecological belt spanning Rajasthan, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, and West Bengal, Indian mustard is prized for its high seed oil concentration ranging from thirty-eight to forty-two percent, its pungent allylisothiocyanate organoleptic profile beloved in North Indian gastronomy, and its remarkable adaptation to conserved residual moisture profiles following kharif fallows or pearl millet harvests. Achieving consistent high yields requires mastering precision planting dates, balanced sulfur nutrition, systemic mitigation of the parasitic weed Orobanche, biological suppression of the mustard aphid, and deliberate harnessing of insect pollination synergies.

The phenological trajectory of Indian mustard is acutely governed by ambient temperature during emergence and reproductive flowering, rendering sowing date calibration the foremost agronomic determinant of crop performance. The optimal sowing window across the semi-arid northwestern plains extends from October tenth to October twenty-fifth. Sowing earlier in early October exposes emerging germinating seedlings to high surface soil temperatures exceeding thirty-four degrees Celsius, causing widespread hypocotyl mortality, severe seedling wilt, and intense early attacks by the painted bug, Bagrada hilaris. Conversely, delaying sowing beyond the first week of November severely impairs vegetative dry matter accumulation, shortens the duration of the reproductive branch development phase, and pushes flowering and siliqua elongation directly into the severe aphid epidemics and warm desiccating winds of late winter. Seedbed preparation requires two shallow passes with a cultivator followed by planking to create a firm, fine, crumb-structured seedbed that prevents capillary moisture loss. Sowing should be executed using a seed-cum-fertilizer drill at a depth of three to four centimeters, maintaining an optimal seed rate of four to five kilograms per hectare at thirty to forty-five centimeters row spacing. Because the seed is minute, mandatory thinning must be executed twelve to fifteen days after emergence to leave vigorous individual seedlings spaced ten to twelve centimeters apart within the row, establishing an optimal plant population of two hundred and twenty thousand to two hundred and eighty thousand plants per hectare.

Nutrient management in Brassica juncea requires balanced application of macronutrients complemented by non-negotiable sulfur fertilization. A high-yielding irrigated mustard crop demands eighty to one hundred kilograms of nitrogen, forty kilograms of phosphorus pentoxide, and forty kilograms of potassium oxide per hectare. Because nitrogen volatilization and leaching are rapid in sandy loam tracts, half the nitrogen along with the entire phosphorus and potassium doses should be applied basal below the seed line, with the remaining nitrogen top-dressed at the first irrigation during the rosette branching stage, thirty to thirty-five days after sowing. Among secondary nutrients, sulfur is the paramount physiological driver of oil biosynthesis and glucosinolate enzyme pathways. Because oil synthesis in Brassica seeds requires sulfur-rich acetyl-coenzyme A and cysteine amino acid building blocks, sulfur deficiency causes severe intervenal chlorosis, cupping of young leaves, reduced siliquae formation, and an immediate two to four percentage point decline in seed oil content. Applying twenty to forty kilograms of elemental sulfur or two hundred to three hundred kilograms of agricultural gypsum per hectare during final land preparation elevates seed yields by twenty to thirty percent while increasing oil concentration. Two critical irrigations—the first at thirty to thirty-five days after sowing (rosette branching stage) and the second at sixty to sixty-five days after sowing (siliqua pod-filling stage)—sustain cell turgor and maximize grain filling.

A profound agronomic and biological multiplier in mustard production is the deliberate integration of managed honeybee pollination. Although Indian mustard flowers possess both functional stamens and pistils and are capable of autonomous self-pollination, the floral architecture—featuring open, nectar-rich cruciform corollas and prominent nectary glands—is evolutionarily specialized for entomophilous cross-pollination. Field research coordinated by the Directorate of Rapeseed-Mustard Research at Bharatpur proves that placing three to five active hives of Apis mellifera or native Apis cerana indica colonies per hectare inside or adjacent to flowering mustard fields increases seed set by fifteen to twenty-five percent. Honeybees foraging for golden pollen and sweet nectar brush against anthers and transfer viable pollen grains across adjacent plants and cross-rows. This intensive cross-pollination ensures synchronous fertilization of ovules, enhances siliqua pod length, increases the number of well-filled seeds per pod from ten to over sixteen, reduces pod shattering, and elevates the thousand-grain test weight. Furthermore, the integration of apiculture provides farmers with a high-value secondary revenue stream through harvested raw mustard honey, which crystalizes rapidly into a creamy white table spread commanding premium market prices. To protect these invaluable pollinators, farmers must strictly prohibit the daytime spraying of toxic insecticides during the four-week flowering bloom, confining any essential plant protection sprays strictly to late twilight hours after honeybee flight activity has ceased.

Biotic constraints in mustard cultivation are headlined by the destructive parasitic angiosperm Orobanche aegyptiaca, commonly known as broomrape. Orobanche is an obligate root parasite lacking chlorophyll that attacks Brassica roots in coarse, light-textured soils across Rajasthan and Haryana. The microscopic seeds of Orobanche remain dormant and viable in soil for over a decade, germinating only when stimulated by strigolactone chemical signals exuded by host mustard roots. The emerging parasite produces specialized subterranean haustoria that penetrate the host vascular cambium, siphoning water, mineral nutrients, and carbohydrates directly from the mustard xylem and phloem, producing severe host stunting, leaf scorching, and twenty to seventy percent yield losses. Effective Orobanche management requires an integrated approach: executing deep summer plowing to invert soil strata; rotating infested plots with non-host trap crops such as pearl millet, sorghum, or cluster bean; and executing precision chemical suppression. Research from the Directorate of Rapeseed-Mustard Research confirms that applying low-dose glyphosate at twenty-five to thirty grams of active ingredient per hectare as a directed foliar spray between mustard rows at thirty and fifty-five days after sowing systemically translocates into Orobanche haustoria without causing host injury, suppressing parasitic spike emergence.

Foliar insect pests and fungal diseases demand disciplined Integrated Pest Management. The mustard aphid, Lipaphis erysimi, represents the most devastating insect threat, multiplying exponentially during cold, cloudy, overcast weather in January. Enormous colonies of green nymphs and winged adults cluster along tender terminal shoots, inflorescences, and siliquae, sucking cell sap and excreting copious sugary honeydew that fosters black sooty mold, reducing photosynthesis and causing complete pod abortion. Chemical intervention is warranted when field scouting detects the economic threshold level of twenty-five to thirty aphids per ten-centimeter terminal inflorescence on ten percent of sampled plants, deploying systemic sprays such as dimethoate 30 EC at one milliliter per liter or thiamethoxam 25 WG at zero point two grams per liter. Among fungal pathogens, white rust, caused by the oomycete Albugo candida, produces prominent chalky-white pustules on the undersides of leaves and induces the dramatic staghead floral malformation, wherein infected inflorescences swell into sterile, contorted green structures. Cultivating modern disease-resistant varieties released by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research, such as DRMR 150-35, Giriraj, NRCHB 101, and RH 725, combined with prophylactic seed treatment with metalaxyl at two grams per kilogram and timely mancozeb sprays, preserves foliar health and ensures full yield expression.

The enterprise economics of Indian mustard establish it as an exceptionally profitable rabi cash crop delivering robust returns on modest working capital. Sowing improved certified cultivars under recommended scientific packages requires paid-out cash cultivation expenses of eighteen thousand to twenty-four thousand rupees per hectare, covering land preparation, certified seed, basal fertilizers, sulfur amendments, and two light irrigations. Delivering grain yields between one point eight and two point five tonnes per hectare under irrigated management, mustard commands strong market demand driven by domestic edible oil consumption and Minimum Support Prices exceeding five thousand six hundred rupees per quintal. Gross revenues range between one hundred thousand and one hundred and forty thousand rupees per hectare, generating net operating margins of eighty thousand to one hundred and fifteen thousand rupees per hectare across a four-month winter window. When combined with apicultural honey sales and the break-crop effect that interrupts cereal root diseases before the succeeding kharif crop, mustard establishes itself as an indispensable pillar of agrarian profitability and national edible oil security.
