# Soil Contamination, Salinity, Previous Land Use and Remediation

The biological health of agricultural soil is a fragile ecological balance built over centuries of organic biomass accumulation, weathering, and microbial colonization. However, soil can be catastrophically degraded in a matter of months through toxic agrochemical deposition, industrial effluent contamination, or severe secondary salinization. An agriculturalist evaluating prospective farmland cannot rely upon visual greenery or surface weed vigor, as aggressive halophytic weeds routinely thrive across chemically toxic soils. Neglecting to investigate historical land use patterns and failing to conduct exhaustive laboratory chemical profiles exposes an enterprise to severe agronomic failure. Understanding the distinct chemical classifications of salt-affected soils, deploying scientifically validated chemical and biological reclamation protocols, and diagnosing prior industrial and excavation damage is vital for rehabilitating degraded land into commercially productive acreage.

## The Chemical Taxonomy: Saline, Sodic and Saline-Sodic Soils

Salt-affected soils represent one of the most widespread land degradation challenges across arid, semi-arid, and canal-irrigated alluvial plains. Proper remediation demands precise chemical classification based on three foundational laboratory parameters: electrical conductivity, soil reaction pH, and the exchangeable sodium percentage. A saline soil is characterized by an electrical conductivity exceeding four deciSiemens per meter, a soil pH below eight point five, and an exchangeable sodium percentage below fifteen percent. In saline soils, high concentrations of neutral soluble salts—predominantly chlorides and sulfates of sodium, calcium, and magnesium—create extreme osmotic pressure in the soil solution. This osmotic stress prevents plant roots from absorbing water even when the soil profile is visibly moist, effectively subjecting the crop to physiological drought.

Conversely, a sodic or alkali soil—traditionally termed usar or kallar across northern India—presents an entirely different and far more destructive physical and chemical pathology. Sodic soils exhibit an electrical conductivity below four deciSiemens per meter, an extremely alkaline pH exceeding eight point five and frequently reaching nine point five to ten, and an exchangeable sodium percentage exceeding fifteen percent. In sodic environments, an excess of exchangeable sodium ions dominates the negative surface charges of clay minerals, forcing clay particles to disperse and hydrate. Soil structure completely collapses into a dense, structureless, cement-like matrix. Upon wetting, sodic soils become slaked and impermeable; upon drying, they bake into rock-hard crusts exhibiting massive hexagonal surface fissures. Water infiltration drops to zero, air diffusion ceases, and caustic sodium carbonate dissolves organic humus, depositing a characteristic black surface scum historically termed black alkali.

A third category, saline-sodic soils, combines high soluble salt concentrations with an exchangeable sodium percentage exceeding fifteen percent. These soils require sophisticated, sequential chemical intervention; attempting to leach a saline-sodic soil with fresh water without first applying chemical calcium amendments invariably strips away soluble electrolytes, causing the soil to instantly collapse into an impenetrable sodic state.

## Chemical Reclamation Protocols: The CSSRI Gypsum Technology Package

Rehabilitating sodic usar soils requires displacing toxic exchangeable sodium ions from the clay exchange complex and replacing them with beneficial divalent calcium ions. Pioneered by the Central Soil Salinity Research Institute in Karnal, Haryana, the standardized gypsum technology package represents one of the most successful land reclamation frameworks in agricultural science.

The process begins by conducting laboratory soil tests to determine the exact Gypsum Requirement, expressed in metric tonnes per hectare. Agricultural-grade mineral gypsum, comprising calcium sulfate dihydrate, is broadcast uniformly across the field at rates typically ranging between ten and fifteen tonnes per hectare and incorporated into the upper ten to fifteen centimeters of the soil profile using a light disk harrow. Concurrently, deep subsoiling or tractor chiseling must be executed to mechanically shatter dense subterranean kankar hardpans, which are impermeable layers of calcium carbonate that physically impede drainage.

Following gypsum incorporation, the field must be divided into level, bunded plots and subjected to continuous water ponding for ten to fifteen days. The applied water dissolves the gypsum, releasing divalent calcium ions that bond strongly to clay particles, displacing monovalent sodium ions into the soil solution. The displaced sodium reacts with sulfate radicals to form highly soluble sodium sulfate, which is subsequently leached deep below the root zone by percolating water or evacuated through subsurface tile drainage networks. Once soil pH drops below eight point two and the exchangeable sodium percentage falls below fifteen percent, the soil profile regains its natural aggregate crumb structure, restoring hydraulic conductivity and aeration.

## Biological Amelioration and Organic Amendments

Chemical gypsum application must be immediately followed by biological green manuring to re-establish soil organic carbon, activate dormant microbial populations, and buffer residual alkalinity. The gold standard in biological reclamation across South Asia is Dhaincha, botanically classified as Sesbania aculeata. Dhaincha is a remarkably salt-tolerant, fast-growing leguminous plant capable of flourishing in harsh, partially reclaimed sodic soils.

Sown immediately following gypsum leaching, Dhaincha produces immense vegetative biomass within forty-five to fifty-five days, fixing atmospheric nitrogen through Rhizobium nodules. At the onset of flowering, the standing crop is chopped and incorporated directly into the soil using a heavy tractor-mounted rotavator. As the succulent green biomass decomposes under warm, moist conditions, heterotrophic bacteria release large quantities of organic acids, including citric, oxalic, and malic acids, alongside carbon dioxide. This respiratory carbon dioxide dissolves in soil water to form carbonic acid, which naturally reacts with native insoluble soil calcium carbonate, releasing fresh native calcium to accelerate further sodium displacement. Supplementing green manuring with industrial sugarcane pressmud, well-rotted farmyard manure, and pyrolyzed agricultural biochar dramatically elevates cation exchange capacity, suppresses soil crusting, and provides a fertile seedbed capable of supporting salt-tolerant cereal cultivars, such as CSR-30 basmati rice and KRL-210 wheat.

## Prior Land Use Hazards: Brick Kilns and Peri-Urban Industrial Toxicity

Diagnosing site suitability requires auditing historical human land use across preceding decades. A catastrophic land degradation hazard common across peri-urban agricultural corridors is prior utilization for commercial brick manufacturing kilns, locally termed bhattas. Traditional brick manufacturing strips away thirty to one hundred centimeters of fertile, organic-rich A-horizon topsoil to mold clay bricks, leaving behind deeply excavated, depressed craters. When abandoned brick kilns are leveled and offered for agricultural lease or sale, purchasers inherit an exposed, infertile subsoil characterized by severe compaction, high bulk density, zero microbial life, and complete nitrogen and phosphorus starvation. Rebuilding the agricultural fertility of an excavated brick kiln site requires years of continuous heavy manure applications, laser land re-grading, deep subsoil ripping, and intensive green manuring before standard crop yields can be achieved.

Equally insidious is soil contamination resulting from peri-urban industrial effluent irrigation. Agricultural parcels situated downstream from textile dyeing clusters, chemical manufacturing parks, or municipal sewage drains have frequently been irrigated for decades with wastewater laden with toxic heavy metals, including cadmium, lead, hexavalent chromium, and nickel. While industrial wastewater contains dissolved nitrogen that produces deceptively lush, dark green foliage, heavy metals permanently accumulate in the soil profile and translocate directly into the edible tissues of leafy vegetables, root crops, and grains. Cultivating produce on heavy-metal-contaminated soils poses severe human toxicity hazards, guarantees regulatory rejection under Food Safety and Standards Authority of India food residue standards, and exposes the enterprise to devastating commercial brand destruction. Verifying historical land use, demanding comprehensive heavy-metal laboratory assays, and applying disciplined soil reclamation science protects an agricultural enterprise from investing capital in toxic or degraded lands that cannot sustain safe, profitable commercial food production.
