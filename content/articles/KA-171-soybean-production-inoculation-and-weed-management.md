# Soybean Production, Inoculation and Weed Management

Soybean, classified botanically as Glycine max, functions as the supreme dual-purpose industrial commodity within modern agricultural commerce, bridging the critical global markets for edible vegetable oil and high-protein animal feed. Originating in East Asia and successfully adapted across the rainfed black soil landscapes of central and peninsular India, soybean has transformed agrarian economics across Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Karnataka, and Telangana. Unlike fine cereals whose primary commercial value resides in endosperm starch, the soybean seed represents a dense biological matrix containing eighteen to twenty percent high-quality edible oil alongside thirty-eight to forty-two percent complete plant protein rich in essential amino acids like lysine. However, achieving dependable, high-yielding soybean production on the heavy, swelling black Vertisols of central India presents formidable agronomic hurdles. Success requires overcoming acute drainage vulnerabilities through specialized land configuration, ensuring biological nodulation through host-specific rhizobial inoculation, and executing rigorous, chemical-integrated weed suppression during the critical early canopy establishment phase.

The predominant ecological domain of Indian soybean production is anchored within the extensive black clay Vertisols and associated Inceptisols of the Malwa plateau, Vidarbha, and Marathwada. While these deep clay soils possess exceptional moisture-retention capacities and high native fertility, their physical behavior presents extreme structural challenges during the kharif monsoon. Vertisols contain high proportions of expanding montmorillonite and smectite clay minerals that swell rapidly upon hydration and shrink upon drying. During prolonged, torrential monsoon downpours, surface clay aggregates disperse, pore spaces become fully water-saturated, and internal hydraulic conductivity drops to near zero. Sowing soybean on conventional flat seedbeds under these conditions frequently leads to severe waterlogging, anaerobic root hypoxia, hypocotyl damping-off, and widespread stand collapse. Conversely, when monsoon breaks occur in August, these heavy clays dry rapidly from the surface downward, forming deep, gaping vertical shrinkage cracks that tear lateral root networks and accelerate subsoil moisture evaporation.

To permanently conquer the dual hazards of waterlogging and mid-season drought in Vertisols, the Indian Institute of Soybean Research at Indore champions the adoption of the Broad Bed Furrow land management system. Sowing is executed using tractor-mounted broad bed furrow seed-cum-fertilizer drills that configure the field into raised, consolidated beds one hundred and twenty to one hundred and fifty centimeters wide, separated by thirty-centimeter-wide drainage furrows fifteen centimeters deep. Four to five crop rows are drilled along the top of each raised bed at thirty to forty-five centimeters row spacing. The Broad Bed Furrow configuration delivers extraordinary dual-purpose hydrological buffering: during torrential monsoon rainstorms, excess gravitational water drains rapidly off the raised beds into the furrows and flows harmlessly toward grassed field waterways, preventing waterlogging, preserving root zone aeration, and keeping seedling crowns elevated above standing water. Conversely, during extended mid-season dry spells lasting three to four weeks, the deep furrows act as in-situ rainwater harvesting reservoirs, trapping localized runoff and channeling capillary moisture directly into the root zones of adjacent beds, while simultaneously permitting life-saving furrow irrigation from on-farm farm ponds. Agronomic trials consistently demonstrate that broad bed furrow establishment elevates soybean grain yields by twenty to thirty-five percent over conventional flat-sowing while insulating crops against erratic monsoon volatility.

Biological nitrogen fixation in soybean is mediated through a strict, host-specific symbiotic mutualism with the bacterium Bradyrhizobium japonicum and Bradyrhizobium elkanii. Because soybean is an introduced exotic legume in the Indian subcontinent, indigenous tropical soils frequently lack native populations of effective Bradyrhizobium japonicum strains. Planting un-inoculated soybean seed into new or rotated fields leads to pale, chlorotic, nitrogen-starved plants that produce poor yields and fail to nodulate. To guarantee maximum nitrogen fixation, certified seed must be inoculated with high-potency, host-specific Bradyrhizobium japonicum culture combined with phosphate-solubilizing bacterial inoculants such as Bacillus megaterium or Pseudomonas striata at five grams per kilogram of seed each. Seed inoculation must strictly follow the fungicide-insecticide-biofertilizer sequence: chemical seed dressing with carbendazim plus thiram at two grams per kilogram must be applied first to suppress seed-borne fungal blights; once the seed has dried in shade, the bacterial slurry—suspended in a ten percent jaggery solution that serves as an adhesive and osmotic protectant—is coated evenly onto the seeds immediately prior to drilling. A well-nodulated soybean stand fixes between eighty and one hundred and fifty kilograms of atmospheric nitrogen per hectare, satisfying over seventy percent of the crop's immense nitrogen requirements and leaving thirty to forty kilograms of residual organic nitrogen in the soil for the succeeding rabi wheat or chickpea crop. Chemical fertilization is confined to a basal starter dose of twenty to thirty kilograms of nitrogen, sixty to eighty kilograms of phosphorus pentoxide, and forty kilograms of potassium oxide per hectare, drilled five centimeters below the seed line at planting.

Weed competition represents the single most aggressive biological factor depressing soybean yields in central India, frequently inflicting catastrophic grain losses between forty and eighty percent if neglected. In warm, moist Vertisols, aggressive broadleaf weeds like Trianthema portulacastrum, Celosia argentea, and Digitaria sanguinalis, alongside competitive sedges like Cyperus rotundus, emerge synchronously with the crop, outgrowing young soybean seedlings within ten days. The critical period of weed competition spans between fifteen and forty-five days after sowing. During this critical window, continuous monsoon rains frequently keep the black clay soil in a sticky, unworkable condition, completely preventing tractors from operating inter-cultivation blade harrows and barring manual labor from entering the muddy fields. Consequently, chemical weed suppression serves as an indispensable technological cornerstone of commercial soybean agronomy.

An effective Integrated Weed Management program in soybean combines pre-emergence residual herbicide applications with targeted early post-emergence chemistries approved by the Central Insecticides Board and Registration Committee. Within forty-eight hours of sowing on moist soil, farmers apply pre-emergence herbicides such as pendimethalin 30 EC at one point zero kilogram of active ingredient per hectare or diclosulam 84 WDG at twenty-six grams of active ingredient per hectare, which forms a chemical barrier on the soil surface that suppresses germinating weed seedlings for twenty to twenty-five days. If prolonged rainfall delays pre-emergence spraying or persistent weeds escape, early post-emergence herbicides must be applied between fifteen and twenty days after sowing when weeds are small, at the two-to-three-leaf stage. Deploying selective broad-spectrum post-emergence herbicides—such as imazethapyr 10 SL at one hundred grams of active ingredient per hectare or a premix combination of imazamox plus imazethapyr—effectively eliminates both broadleaf weeds and annual grasses without causing phytotoxic injury to the soybean crop.

The commercial macro-economics of the soybean enterprise are uniquely governed by the valuation of De-Oiled Cake, also known as soybean meal, which dictates global and domestic processing margins. Unlike traditional oilseed crops like mustard or groundnut, where the extracted vegetable oil constitutes sixty to seventy percent of total commodity value, soybean processing operates under the exact reverse economic paradigm. In a standard industrial solvent-extraction crushing plant, one hundred kilograms of clean raw soybean yields approximately eighteen kilograms of crude degummed soybean oil and eighty kilograms of high-protein De-Oiled Cake. Consequently, soybean meal accounts for nearly seventy percent of the total commercial revenue generated by the crop, serving as the premier, non-substitutable protein meal consumed by national and global poultry, aquaculture, and livestock feed industries. Supported by robust domestic feed demand, strong meal exports, and statutory Minimum Support Prices exceeding four thousand eight hundred rupees per quintal, a well-managed broad-bed furrow soybean crop yielding two point zero to two point five tonnes per hectare generates gross revenues of ninety-five thousand to one hundred and twenty thousand rupees per hectare against cultivation costs of twenty-two thousand to twenty-eight thousand rupees. By integrating precision drainage engineering, certified biological inoculation, and scientific weed management, soybean producers establish a profitable, high-value summer cash crop that anchors industrial feed value chains and revitalizes rainfed Vertisol agriculture across India.
