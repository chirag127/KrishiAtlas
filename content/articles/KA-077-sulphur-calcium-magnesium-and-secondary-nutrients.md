# Sulphur, Calcium, Magnesium and Secondary Nutrients

In historical agricultural terminology, the classification of sulfur, calcium, and magnesium as secondary nutrients created a disastrous and misleading impression among farm managers that these elements were of secondary importance to crop survival and farm profitability. In modern plant physiology, this semantic distinction has been completely overturned. Higher plants absorb secondary nutrients in quantities comparable to, and in certain oilseed and brassica crops exceeding, their physiological uptake of elemental phosphorus. Far from playing peripheral roles, sulfur, calcium, and magnesium govern the fundamental structural framework of plant cell walls, drive the central light-harvesting machinery of photosynthesis, and synthesize the sulfur-bearing amino acids that determine dietary protein quality. Over the past four decades, the relentless focus on primary nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium fertilization has triggered catastrophic secondary nutrient depletion across arable soils worldwide, turning secondary nutrient starvation into the single most widespread unaddressed yield barrier in tropical and sub-tropical agriculture.

The emergence of widespread sulfur deficiency across Indian agriculture represents a classic textbook case of unintended agronomic consequences stemming from fertilizer industrialization. Prior to the late nineteen-seventies, Indian farmers routinely applied low-analysis fertilizers, principally single superphosphate, which contains sixteen percent phosphorus pentoxide alongside eleven to twelve percent sulfur and nineteen percent calcium, and ammonium sulphate, delivering twenty percent nitrogen and twenty-four percent sulfur. Furthermore, modest industrial emissions deposited incidental sulfur dioxide across surrounding countryside soils via atmospheric precipitation. Under this regime, crops received incidental but generous sulfur replenishment with every fertilizer bag spread upon the land.

The subsequent policy-driven modernization of the fertilizer sector triggered a massive transition toward high-analysis, sulfur-free chemical commodities, dominated by diammonium phosphate delivering eighteen percent nitrogen and forty-six percent phosphorus pentoxide, alongside prilled urea delivering forty-six percent nitrogen and muriate of potash delivering sixty percent potassium oxide. While these concentrated carriers slashed bulk transportation and handling costs, they delivered exactly zero sulfur to the soil. As intensive high-yielding cropping systems expanded, multiple harvests continuously stripped the soil profile of native sulfur reserves without replenishment. Surveys conducted by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research through the All India Coordinated Research Project on Micro- and Secondary-Nutrients reveal that over forty-five percent of agricultural districts across India now suffer from acute, yield-limiting sulfur deficiencies.

This sulfur deficit has devastated the economic productivity of oilseed and pulse cultivation. Sulfur is an indispensable building block for the synthesis of the essential amino acids methionine and cysteine, which cross-link polypeptide chains through disulfide bonds to form functional proteins. In oilseeds—including rapeseed-mustard, groundnut, soybean, and sunflower—sulfur is directly required for the enzymatic biosynthesis of triglycerides, fatty acids, and glucosinolates. When sulfur is deficient, oil concentrations plummet, seed weights collapse, and nitrogen applied as urea cannot be converted into protein, instead accumulating as toxic non-protein nitrate within vegetative tissues. Furthermore, sulfur is an essential component of ferredoxin and the nitrogenase enzyme complex within symbiotic Rhizobium bacteria in pulse crops; sulfur-starved pulses exhibit pale, inactive root nodules and severely impaired atmospheric nitrogen fixation. Because sulfate behaves as a mobile anion susceptible to leaching in light soils, corrective management relies on applying agricultural-grade gypsum, single superphosphate, or slow-release bentonite-sulfur pastilles, ensuring sustained sulfate availability throughout the vegetative and pod-filling phases.

Calcium operates under an entirely unique physiological regime governed by vascular mechanics and cellular architecture. Within the plant, calcium functions primarily as a structural cementing agent, binding with pectic acids to form calcium pectate within the middle lamella, which glues adjacent cell walls together and confers structural rigidity to stems, leaves, and fruits. Additionally, calcium maintains the integrity of cellular plasma membranes and functions as a ubiquitous secondary messenger within the cytoplasm, transducing external environmental cues into adaptive physiological responses.

Unlike nitrogen or potassium, calcium is completely immobile within the plant phloem vascular system. Once deposited into older vegetative tissues, calcium cannot be remobilized or translocated to support newly emerging organs. Calcium moves exclusively through the xylem vessels, driven entirely by the passive mass flow of water pulled upward by transpirational pull. This absolute dependence on transpiration creates severe, localized calcium deficiencies in plant organs that possess low transpirational rates, regardless of how much calcium resides within the soil profile. Developing fruits, enclosed cabbage hearts, and shaded apical buds possess microscopic stomatal densities and high relative humidity, resulting in negligible transpiration compared to expansive, sun-exposed outer canopy leaves.

Consequently, during periods of rapid vegetative growth or sudden hot, dry weather, transpirational water is pulled almost entirely toward the outer leaves, starving the developing reproductive sinks of calcium. This localized failure of cell wall development triggers devastating physiological disorders: blossom end rot in tomatoes, peppers, and eggplants, where the distal tissue of the fruit collapses into a black, sunken necrotic lesion; bitter pit in apples, marked by corky, bitter spots beneath the skin; tip burn in lettuce and strawberries; and hollow heart or unfilled pods, known as pops, in groundnuts. Preventing these disorders requires managing the plant microclimate, maintaining consistent soil moisture to prevent root xylem cavitation, and deploying targeted foliar sprays of chelated calcium directly onto developing flower clusters and fruitlets, bypassing the transpirational bypass.

Magnesium occupies the chemical throne of plant photosynthesis, serving as the central coordinating metallic atom within the porphyrin ring of the chlorophyll molecule, mirroring the position of iron in human hemoglobin. Approximately fifteen to twenty percent of total plant magnesium is sequestered within chlorophyll; without adequate magnesium, light absorption, electron transport, and photochemical carbon assimilation cease. The remaining eighty percent of cellular magnesium resides within the cytoplasm and chloroplast stroma, where it acts as an obligate catalytic cofactor for every adenosine triphosphate-dependent enzyme in plant biology. Magnesium bridges the negatively charged phosphate groups of adenosine triphosphate molecules, enabling kinase enzymes to transfer high-energy phosphate bonds during glycolysis, respiration, and protein synthesis. Because magnesium is highly mobile within the phloem, deficiency symptoms manifest as striking, symmetrical interveinal chlorosis across older, lower leaves, where the leaf veins remain dark green while the intervening mesophyll tissue degrades into brilliant yellow, orange, or bronze hues as the plant cannibalizes older foliage to sustain terminal shoot growth.

Effective secondary nutrient management is perpetually complicated by the intense chemical phenomenon of cation competition and antagonism. Calcium, magnesium, and potassium are all positively charged cations that compete for the same negative electrostatic adsorption sites on soil clay-humus complexes and vie for the same carrier proteins and ion channels within root epidermal membranes. Indiscriminate or unbalanced fertilization disrupts this delicate equilibrium. When farmers apply massive, uncontrolled doses of calcitic limestone or agricultural gypsum to sodic soils, the flood of free divalent calcium cations into the soil solution aggressively displaces and suppresses the uptake of divalent magnesium, inducing severe magnesium starvation in crops growing on soils with moderate native magnesium reserves. Similarly, excessive applications of muriate of potash inundate root ion channels with monovalent potassium cations, which outcompete magnesium for root absorption. Sustaining high-yielding crop canopies requires balancing the soil cation exchange complex, ensuring that calcium, magnesium, and potassium occupy harmonious saturation percentages, supported by periodic applications of dolomitic limestone or magnesium sulphate, turning secondary nutrition into primary farm profit.
