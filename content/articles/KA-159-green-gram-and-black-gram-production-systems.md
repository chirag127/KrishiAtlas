# Green Gram and Black Gram Production Systems

Green gram, designated botanically as Vigna radiata and known across South Asia as moong, alongside black gram, classified as Vigna mungo and celebrated as urd or mash, constitute the two most agronomically flexible and culinarily indispensable short-duration grain legumes cultivated in India. Indigenous to the Indian subcontinent, these twin Vigna species possess an extraordinarily compressed phenology, completing their seed-to-seed reproductive life cycle within fifty-five to seventy-five days. Because of this rapid turnover, green gram and black gram serve as premier catch crops, intercrops, and rotational break crops capable of fitting neatly into temporal niches between major staple cereal harvests. Whether cultivated during the primary kharif monsoon, broadcast as zero-till relay crops into coastal rabi rice fallows, or planted as intensive summer catch crops across the irrigated northern plains, green gram and black gram restore depleted soil nitrogen, disrupt cereal pest and disease cycles, and provide high-protein nutrition and rapid cash flow to farming enterprises.

The production architecture of green gram and black gram is differentiated into three distinct seasonal cultivation windows: main kharif, rabi rice-fallow, and spring-summer catch cropping. Kharif cultivation coincides with the southwest monsoon, sown across Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Karnataka in late June or early July. While kharif crops benefit from abundant monsoon precipitation, they face substantial biological hazards, including foliar fungal blights, pod-sucking bugs, and late-season rains that cause mature pods to shatter and grain to sprout inside the pod before harvest. In southern and coastal eastern India—encompassing Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, and Odisha—black gram and green gram dominate the post-monsoon rabi season through the traditional relay practice known as paira or utera cropping. In these deltaic systems, pulse seed is broadcast directly into standing lowland paddy fields ten to fifteen days prior to rice harvest while the soil remains saturated. The emerging pulse seedlings establish root systems without any mechanical tillage, utilizing residual subsoil moisture after the rice canopy is reaped and maturing cleanly on conserved winter moisture without requiring synthetic fertilizers or supplemental irrigation.

The most revolutionary agronomic development in Vigna cultivation is the dramatic expansion of spring and summer catch cropping across the Indo-Gangetic Plains of Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, and Bihar. Historically, agricultural fields lay barren and fallow for seventy to eighty days between the harvest of rabi winter crops (such as wheat, potato, or mustard) in late March and the transplanting of kharif paddy in late June. Farmers now exploit this intense summer window by drilling short-duration green gram (summer moong) immediately following wheat harvest. Cultivated under bright, unclouded solar radiation and low atmospheric humidity, summer moong experiences virtually zero foliar fungal infection. However, successful summer cultivation requires strict adherence to planting calendars: sowing must occur between March twentieth and April tenth. Sowing delayed into late April or May pushes flowering into the severe, desiccating dry winds (loo) of late spring, which desiccate floral stigmas, reduce pollen viability, and increase the hazard that late-maturing pods will be trapped and ruined by early pre-monsoon convective thunderstorms in mid-June.

A decisive breeding breakthrough that transformed commercial Vigna production was the transition from traditional indeterminate, sprawling landraces to modern determinate cultivars possessing synchronous maturity and non-shattering pod characteristics. Historically, traditional green gram and black gram varieties flowered continuously over four to six weeks, requiring three to four separate manual hand pickings of ripe black pods as lower pods matured while upper branches were still flowering. This high labor dependency rendered commercial pulse farming unprofitable as rural daily wage rates escalated. National pulse breeders at the Indian Institute of Pulses Research and state agricultural universities developed elite determinate cultivars—such as IPM 205-7 (Virat), IPM 410-3 (Shikha), SML 668, Samrat, and Pusa Vishal in green gram, and IPU 2-43 (Uttara) and Pant U-31 in black gram—that concentrate ninety percent of their flowering within a narrow seven-day window. All pods mature concurrently, and the plants hold their pods erect above the foliar canopy without dehiscing or shattering, enabling single-pass manual harvesting using hand sickles or mechanical harvesting using modified combine harvesters.

The supreme pathological threat to both green gram and black gram across all ecological zones is Mungbean Yellow Mosaic Virus, a devastating begomovirus transmitted exclusively by the whitefly vector Bemisia tabaci. In susceptible cultivars, initial symptoms manifest as tiny, chlorotic yellow specks on young leaves, which rapidly expand and coalesce into irregular bright-yellow mosaic patches intermingled with dark green tissue. In severe systemic infections, the entire leaf lamina turns golden-yellow, apical leaf expansion ceases, plants become severely stunted, and flower buds abort completely, inflicting catastrophic yield losses between seventy and one hundred percent. Because chemical viricides do not exist, managing Mungbean Yellow Mosaic Virus requires an integrated strategy: sowing certified seed of genetically resistant varieties bred specifically for whitefly tolerance; treating seed prior to planting with systemic neonicotinoid insecticides such as imidacloprid 70 WS at five grams or thiamethoxam 30 FS at five milliliters per kilogram of seed to protect seedlings during the hyper-susceptible first twenty-five days; planting dense perimeter border rows of sorghum or pearl millet to physically impede wind-borne whitefly vectors from landing in the pulse field; and spot-spraying systemic vector insecticides at the first appearance of whiteflies to prevent secondary viral transmission.

Agronomic input management in green gram and black gram requires careful calibration to avoid excessive vegetative growth at the expense of reproductive pod formation. Because Vigna roots form highly effective symbiotic associations with native and inoculated Bradyrhizobium bacteria, the crop fixes between thirty-five and fifty kilograms of atmospheric nitrogen per hectare. Applying excessive synthetic nitrogenous fertilizers suppresses nodule initiation and stimulates lush, leafy vegetative canopies that lodge easily and attract insect pests. The recommended fertilizer application is strictly restricted to a starter dose of twenty kilograms of nitrogen and forty to fifty kilograms of phosphorus pentoxide per hectare, placed five centimeters below the seed line at planting. Phosphorus is critical for fueling the high adenosine triphosphate energy demands of nitrogenase enzymes within root nodules and promoting vigorous early root elongation. In summer cultivation, providing three to four light irrigations—timed specifically at the pre-flowering branch elongation stage and the early pod-filling stage—maintains cellular turgor and prevents premature flower drop.

The enterprise economics of summer moong and rabi black gram reveal an extraordinary return on investment that maximizes whole-farm annual net profitability. Sowing summer moong at a seed rate of twenty-five to thirty kilograms per hectare incurs total paid-out production costs of sixteen thousand to twenty-two thousand rupees per hectare, covering land preparation, certified seed, starter fertilizer, two weedings, and three tube-well irrigations. Delivering grain yields between one point zero and one point five tonnes per hectare within sixty days, summer moong commands the highest Minimum Support Price among all major pulses, exceeding eight thousand five hundred rupees per quintal. At prevailing wholesale market rates, gross revenues range between eighty-five thousand and one hundred and twenty-five thousand rupees per hectare, generating sixty thousand to one hundred thousand rupees of clear net profit across a two-month summer window from land that previously generated zero revenue. Simultaneously, incorporating one point five to two point five tonnes of green pulse crop stover back into the soil adds forty kilograms of organic nitrogen and substantial humus, allowing the farmer to reduce nitrogen fertilizer inputs by twenty-five percent in the succeeding kharif paddy or basmati crop while elevating cereal yields by eight to twelve percent, establishing Vigna legumes as indispensable engines of agronomic and financial sustainability.
