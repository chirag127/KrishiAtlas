# Why a Water-Intensive Crop May Be Unsuitable Despite High Prices

In commercial agricultural decision-making, farm managers are routinely blinded by the seductive illusion of gross revenue per hectare. When market prices for a commodity soar, or when the state guarantees lucrative price floors—such as the statutory Fair and Remunerative Price for sugarcane or assured public procurement at Minimum Support Price for summer paddy—cultivators rush to convert their entire landholdings into these high-grossing monocultures. In rural banking and extension circles, planting sugarcane or flooded rice is celebrated as the hallmark of progressive farming, offering predictable bulk cash receipts that dwarf the modest revenues generated by traditional dryland cereals, pulses, and oilseeds. Yet when pursued within water-stressed or semi-arid agro-climatic environments, cultivating water-intensive crops represents a profound economic and ecological trap. Beneath the facade of substantial gross revenues lies a catastrophic burn rate of working capital, escalating debt incurred from deepening tube-wells, terminal crop failure risks, and severe resource destruction. Evaluating crop profitability requires moving beyond crude land productivity metrics to analyze Economic Water Productivity, quantifying the hidden capital depreciation of competitive groundwater mining, and aligning enterprise choice with regional hydrology.

The fundamental flaw in conventional farm accounting is measuring productivity exclusively on a per-hectare land basis while treating irrigation water as an infinite, costless input. In semi-arid regions such as Marathwada and Solapur in Maharashtra, northern Karnataka, or the over-exploited groundwater belts of Punjab and Haryana, the primary biophysical constraint restricting wealth generation is not land area; it is usable water volume. A single hectare of commercial sugarcane requires between two thousand and three thousand millimeters of water across its twelve-month growing cycle, consuming twenty to thirty million liters of water in regions where annual rainfall rarely exceeds five hundred to seven hundred millimeters. Similarly, a single hectare of flooded summer paddy consumes twelve to twenty-five million liters of water, with over two-thirds squandered through bare soil evaporation and deep percolation.

When agricultural economists from the Indian Council of Agricultural Research and the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices evaluate crop choices through the lens of Economic Water Productivity—measuring net financial profit generated per cubic meter of water consumed—the economic hierarchy completely flips. Commercial sugarcane cultivated under conventional flood irrigation generates a meager net economic return of only two to five rupees per cubic meter of water. Flooded paddy delivers approximately three to seven rupees per cubic meter. In stark contrast, low-water pulses, oilseeds, and dryland cereals generate fifteen to twenty-five rupees of net profit per cubic meter of water consumed. High-value horticultural crops managed under pressurized drip fertigation—such as pomegranate, guava, commercial vegetables, and onions—generate between thirty and seventy rupees of net margin per cubic meter of water applied. Allocating twenty million liters of water to a single hectare of sugarcane to capture two hundred thousand rupees of gross revenue represents an immense, irrational opportunity cost; that exact same water volume could sustain five hectares of drip-irrigated commercial horticulture, generating over one million rupees in net profit while preserving local aquifers.

Furthermore, cultivating water-intensive crops in water-scarce basins plunges the grower into an inescapable capital expenditure trap, driven by competitive groundwater mining. As thousands of neighboring farmers simultaneously flood sugarcane and paddy basins, the local water table plummets by several meters annually. To keep the water-guzzling crop alive, the farmer is forced into a predatory race to the bottom: drilling deeper borewells, pushing casing depths from one hundred meters down to two hundred, three hundred, or four hundred meters into fractured crystalline basement rock.

Every new deep borewell demands between one hundred and fifty thousand and three hundred thousand rupees in uncapitalized debt, covering heavy drilling rigs, steel casing pipes, and high-capacity fifteen-to-twenty-horsepower multi-stage submersible pumps. Within two to three seasons, adjacent deeper wells suck the fracture zone dry, rendering the newly drilled borewell completely useless. Motor burnouts multiply as rural electrical grids struggle under the immense load of high-horsepower pumps operating on fluctuating voltages, consuming tens of thousands of rupees in rewinding costs. When these recurring, hidden capital and maintenance expenditures are properly depreciated against crop earnings, the apparent paper profit of the water-intensive enterprise evaporates completely into multi-lakh-rupee cumulative debt.

Beyond capital destruction, water-intensive crops in water-limited basins face the constant, catastrophic hazard of terminal moisture failure. Sugarcane requires massive, continuous water deliveries during the blistering pre-monsoon summer months of April, May, and June, precisely when regional groundwater tables hit their annual nadir and village tanks dry up completely. If an over-extended tube-well collapses or power rosters fail during this peak summer window, the standing cane crop runs out of water.

The physiological consequences are devastating: cane stalks desiccate into hollow, fibrous poles, internal sucrose concentration collapses, and sugar extraction rates plummet. When hauled to the cooperative sugar mill, the cane is severely discounted, downgraded for low recovery, or rejected entirely as unmerchantable trash. The farmer is left with an unsaleable, standing crop that has already consumed millions of liters of water, hundreds of bags of subsidized fertilizer, and an entire year of working capital, inflicting total financial bankruptcy. In flooded summer paddy, a failure of irrigation during the panicle initiation or flowering window induces floret sterility, producing upright, empty panicles that yield zero merchantable grain.

The long-term ecological consequence of forcing water-intensive monocultures onto unsuitable arid ground is secondary soil degradation and environmental collapse. Continuous heavy irrigation with deep, mineralized groundwater inevitably accelerates soil salinization and sodification, flooding topsoils with sodium, chloride, and carbonate ions that destroy soil aggregate stability and choke permeability. Within a decade of intensive sugarcane or paddy flooding, prime arable fields degrade into barren, crusty, saline wastes that cannot support even drought-tolerant crops without massive, expensive chemical reclamation.

Recognizing these systemic crises, forward-thinking agricultural policies are actively incentivizing farmers to abandon water-intensive crops in over-exploited groundwater zones. In Haryana, the landmark Mera Pani Meri Virasat initiative pays participating farmers a direct cash transfer incentive of seven thousand rupees per acre to voluntarily diversify land away from flooded paddy into water-smart alternatives, including grain maize, pulses, cotton, and commercial agroforestry. By coupling government diversification incentives with high-efficiency micro-drip networks, progressive growers align enterprise selection with hydrological reality. Abandoning the dangerous illusion of water-intensive cash crops, agriculturalists build diversified, multi-enterprise portfolios grounded in high Economic Water Productivity, insulating their enterprises against climatic drought, banishing borewell debt, and securing sustainable agricultural prosperity for future generations.
