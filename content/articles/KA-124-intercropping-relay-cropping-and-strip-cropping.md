# Intercropping, Relay Cropping and Strip Cropping

In agricultural resource management, spatial and temporal efficiency dictate the productivity of land and solar radiation. While large-scale industrialized agriculture frequently prioritizes simple monoculture fields to streamline heavy machinery operations, smallholder and resource-conscious farming systems achieve far higher total biological productivity and ecological resilience through multi-species spatial and temporal architectures. By cultivating two or more crop species simultaneously or in tight overlapping succession across the same land parcel, agricultural producers can capture synergistic biological interactions, maximize canopy radiation interception, exploit complementary subterranean rooting zones, and drastically reduce the systemic risk of total harvest failure. Mastering these advanced cropping systems requires a rigorous understanding of the agronomic mechanics governing intercropping, relay cropping, and contour strip cropping.

Intercropping involves growing two or more distinct crop species simultaneously on the same physical field, arranged either in definitive row patterns or in integrated spatial geometries. The fundamental biological principle underpinning successful intercropping is niche complementarity, both across aboveground space and subterranean root zones. Rather than competing destructively for the same narrow resource pool, companion crops are chosen because their differing growth habits, root depths, phenological cycles, and photosynthetic pathways allow them to exploit distinct ecological niches. A classic, highly productive example across semi-arid India is the intercropping of a tall C4 cereal like sorghum or maize with a slow-starting C3 legume like pigeonpea in a structured spatial ratio of two rows of cereal to one row of pigeonpea, or four rows of cereal to two rows of legume.

In this cereal-legume association, spatial and temporal niche separation functions with elegant precision. The fast-growing C4 cereal establishes an upright, erectophile canopy that rapidly shoots upward during the opening six to eight weeks of the monsoon, capturing intense upper solar radiation and mining water and nutrients from the shallow topsoil with its fibrous root system. During this same window, the pigeonpea grows very slowly above ground, creating negligible shading competition for the cereal, while driving a deep, powerful taproot into the subsoil strata beneath the cereal's root zone. When the cereal completes its reproductive grain-filling cycle and is harvested at ninety to one hundred days, the field is suddenly opened to full sunlight. The established pigeonpea, having maintained continuous root development and fixed atmospheric nitrogen, immediately accelerates its vegetative expansion, flowering, and pod development across the autumn months, utilizing residual subsoil moisture that would otherwise evaporate from fallow ground.

To quantitatively evaluate whether an intercropping system delivers a genuine biological advantage over monoculture cultivation, agronomists utilize the Land Equivalent Ratio, universally abbreviated as LER. The Land Equivalent Ratio is defined as the relative land area required under sole cropping to produce the identical yields achieved under intercropping at the same level of management. Mathematically, the Land Equivalent Ratio is calculated as the sum of the partial ratios of each companion crop, where each partial ratio represents the intercropped yield divided by that crop's yield when cultivated as a pure sole crop. When the calculated Land Equivalent Ratio exceeds one, it demonstrates a clear biological yield advantage; an LER value of one-point-three, for example, indicates that a sole-cropping system would require thirty percent more land area to generate the same aggregate yield as the intercrop, proving superior resource-use efficiency.

Beyond pure spatial intercropping operates the high-efficiency temporal framework known as relay cropping. In a relay cropping system, the succeeding crop is planted directly into an established, standing primary crop before the primary crop reaches physiological maturity or harvest. Relay cropping completely eliminates the standard fallow interval and turnaround delays between crops, capitalizing on the remaining weeks of favorable temperatures and residual soil moisture. Across eastern India—encompassing vast tracts of Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Odisha, and Chhattisgarh—relay cropping is preserved through the traditional agro-ecological practice known as paira or utera cropping.

In the paira cropping system, farmers broadcast the seeds of hardy, short-duration pulses—predominantly grass pea, lentil, or black gram—directly into standing, unharvested lowland paddy fields approximately ten to fifteen days prior to the rice harvest. At this critical juncture, the standing paddy soil is in a moist, saturated mud condition, having absorbed the late monsoon rains. The broadcast pulse seeds land directly on the wet mud surface, rapidly imbibing capillary water and extending a vigorous radicle into the soil beneath the protective shade of the golden rice canopy. By the time manual or combine rice harvesting occurs, the pulse seedlings are firmly established. The paddy straw is cut just above the seedling crowns, suddenly exposing the young legumes to full solar radiation. The pulse crop flourishes solely upon the abundant residual moisture of the rice profile, requiring zero diesel expenditure for preparatory tillage, zero pre-sowing irrigation water, and zero synthetic nitrogen fertilizer, transforming what would otherwise become an unproductive, dry winter fallow into a highly profitable, protein-rich pulse harvest.

Strip cropping represents an advanced spatial architecture designed primarily for sloping terrains, erosion-prone watersheds, and wind-swept semi-arid landscapes. In strip cropping, the field is divided into long, relatively narrow bands or strips laid out along the natural contour lines of the landscape, alternating between erosion-permitting crops and erosion-resisting crops. Erosion-permitting crops—such as widely spaced maize, sorghum, or cotton—leave substantial bare soil exposed between rows during early vegetative growth, making the soil highly vulnerable to droplet detachment and torrential runoff erosion. In contrast, erosion-resisting crops consist of dense, low-growing, close-seeded legumes or forages—such as groundnut, cowpea, soybean, or alfalfa—that establish an immediate, continuous biological carpet over the soil surface.

The physical mechanics of contour strip cropping function as living vegetative buffers. When torrential monsoon downpours generate surface runoff across a sloping maize strip, the descending water velocity accelerates, carrying detached topsoil sediments. However, the moment this muddy runoff enters the adjacent, downhill strip of dense groundnut or cowpea, the thick carpet of vegetative stems and fallen leaf mulch acts as a natural physical filter. Runoff velocity is abruptly halted, causing suspended sediment particles to drop out of the water and be trapped securely on the soil surface, while the slowed water infiltrates deeply into the root zone. By rotating the locations of these contour strips across successive seasons, the agricultural producer stabilizes sloping topsoil, builds long-term organic matter, and prevents the formation of destructive field gullies.

While intercropping, relay cropping, and strip cropping deliver immense ecological and biological advantages, their operational execution introduces distinct managerial complexities. In mechanized farming operations, intercropped systems complicate mechanical tillage, combine harvesting, and tractor-mounted boom spraying, particularly when companion crops mature at different calendar dates. Most critically, chemical weed management becomes exceptionally difficult; a broadleaf herbicide safely applied to suppress broadleaf weeds in a primary cereal crop will instantly kill an intercropped legume companion, demanding either intensive manual weeding or precision shielded sprayers. By carefully evaluating machinery compatibility, matching complementary canopy architectures, and respecting herbicide tolerances, the disciplined agricultural enterprise transforms multi-species cropping from a complex ecological concept into an exceptionally profitable, climate-resilient reality.
