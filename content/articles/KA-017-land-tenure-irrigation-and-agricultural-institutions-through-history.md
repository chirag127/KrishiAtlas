# Land Tenure, Irrigation and Agricultural Institutions Through History

Agricultural productivity is never solely a function of soil chemistry, rainfall, and seeds; it is profoundly determined by the institutional framework governing property rights, land tenure security, water allocation, and fiscal extractions. Across the history of South Asia, the structural relationship between the state, the landholding intermediary, and the actual cultivator has evolved through distinct administrative regimes. From the revenue settlements of the Mughal Empire and the predatory fiscal engineering of British colonial rule to post-independence land reforms and groundwater privatization, institutional arrangements have dictated who invested capital, who bore risk, and who retained the economic surplus of the soil.

## Pre-Colonial Agrarian Institutions: Customary Rights and Todar Mal’s Bandobast

In pre-colonial Indian jurisprudence, absolute individual private property in agricultural land, in the modern Western sense of alienable freehold title, did not exist. Instead, land tenure was characterized by overlapping, customary bundles of rights.

The sovereign claimed the right to collect a share of the agricultural produce, known historically as bhaga, to maintain the state administration, military forces, and public infrastructure. However, the hereditary peasant cultivator, known as the khudkasht ryot, enjoyed permanent, heritable possession of their ancestral plot so long as the customary revenue share was paid. Land was plentiful relative to population, and village communities possessed substantial collective autonomy in managing pastures, forests, and irrigation channels.

The administrative formalization of this customary system reached its peak under the Mughal Empire during the reign of Emperor Akbar, orchestrated by his finance minister, Raja Todar Mal. Todar Mal instituted the Zabt or Bandobast revenue system, replacing arbitrary levies with rigorous empirical measurement. Farmland was surveyed using standardized bamboo rods linked with iron rings, known as the jarib. 

Land was classified into four distinct categories based on cultivation intensity: polaj land, which was cultivated continuously every year; parauti land, which was left fallow for a year or two to recover organic fertility; chachar land, fallowed for three to four years; and banjar land, which had remained uncultivated for five years or more. Under the Ain-i-Dahsala reform of 1580, state revenue was assessed at one-third of the average crop yield calculated over the preceding ten years, converted into fixed cash rates based on prevailing local market prices. This provided revenue predictability for the imperial treasury while linking fiscal demand to verified soil productivity.

## Colonial Fiscal Engineering: The Divergent Revenue Settlements

The expansion of the British East India Company in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries fundamentally shattered indigenous agrarian relations, replacing customary shared rights with rigid legal property definitions designed to maximize imperial tax extraction.

In eastern India, Lord Cornwallis enacted the Permanent Settlement of Bengal in 1793. In a radical misapplication of English landlord-tenant law, the colonial state declared the historic tax-collecting intermediaries, known as Zamindars, to be the absolute proprietary owners of the soil, fixing their revenue obligations to the East India Company in perpetuity. Millions of hereditary peasant cultivators were legally reduced overnight to insecure tenants-at-will. Zamindars subjected the peasantry to extortionate rack-renting, illegal extractions known as abwabs, and summary evictions. Because the state's revenue share was fixed permanently, zamindars had zero incentive to invest in capital land improvements, choosing instead to sub-infeudate their estates into dozens of parasitic rent-collecting tiers, plunging Bengal, Bihar, and coastal Odisha into chronic agrarian de-capitalization.

In southern and western India, Thomas Munro implemented the Ryotwari settlement in the Madras and Bombay Presidencies in 1820. Bypassing intermediaries, the colonial administration contracted directly with individual cultivators, known as ryots. While this avoided feudal zamindari oppression, the state itself acted as a ruthless landlord, fixing revenue demands at crushing levels of fifty percent or more of gross produce, subject to upward revision every thirty years. When monsoons failed, rigid cash collection schedules forced peasants to mortgage their land to village moneylenders, known as sahukars, triggering widespread land alienation, bonded labour, and the violent agrarian uprisings of the Deccan Riots of 1875.

In northwestern India, including Punjab and the North-Western Provinces, Holt Mackenzie instituted the Mahalwari system in 1822. Revenue was settled collectively with village communities or co-sharing brotherhoods, known as mahals, operating under joint fiscal responsibility. In Punjab, relatively moderate revenue assessments, combined with massive state investments in the canal colonies, created a commercialized peasant proprietor class that laid the historical groundwork for subsequent agricultural mechanization.

## Post-Independence Land Reforms: Achievements and Evasions

At independence in 1947, India inherited an agrarian economy characterized by extreme land concentration, rampant absenteeism, and crushing rural indebtedness. The newly independent democratic state initiated a comprehensive, four-pronged program of statutory land reform.

The first and most successful reform was the abolition of feudal intermediaries. Across the 1950s, state legislatures enacted Zamindari Abolition Acts, such as the landmark Uttar Pradesh Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act of 1950. These statutes dismantled over twenty million parasitic intermediaries, transferred proprietary bhumidhari rights directly to actual cultivators, and brought over sixty million hectares of uncultivated waste and forest lands under state ownership.

The second thrust focused on tenancy reform, aiming to guarantee security of tenure, regulate exorbitant rents to no more than one-fourth of gross produce, and confer ownership on tenants. While tenancy reforms largely failed in states where landed elites dominated politics, they achieved historic success in West Bengal under Operation Barga, which legally registered over one point five million sharecroppers, and in Kerala, where sweeping legislation transferred land directly to the tiller, dramatically reducing rural poverty.

The third thrust, the imposition of land ceilings, suffered from widespread political sabotage and legal evasion. Landholders exploited statutory loopholes by executing fictitious benami transfers to distant relatives, servants, or fictitious trusts, while deliberately delaying proceedings through decades of civil litigation. Consequently, out of hundreds of millions of cultivated hectares, barely two to three percent of agricultural land was declared surplus and redistributed to landless families.

The fourth major institutional initiative was the consolidation of fragmented agricultural holdings, known as Chakbandi. Under statutes such as the Uttar Pradesh Consolidation of Holdings Act of 1953, the state restructured millions of scattered, tiny parcels into compact, unified blocks called chaks, reserving land for village access roads, irrigation channels, and communal pastures. Where executed with integrity, as in Punjab, Haryana, and Western Uttar Pradesh, consolidation catalyzed private capital investments in tubewells, tractors, and perimeter fencing, serving as the essential institutional prerequisite for the Green Revolution.

## The Transformation of Irrigation Institutions

In parallel with land tenure, the institutions governing water allocation underwent a dramatic historical transition from decentralized community management to centralized state hydraulic bureaucracy, followed by modern private privatization.

For centuries, peninsular and dryland India managed irrigation through autonomous community institutions. In Tamil Nadu, the kudimaramat tradition mandated that all village landholders contribute seasonal manual labour to desilt community irrigation tanks, repair masonry sluices, and maintain field channels. In Maharashtra, the centuries-old phad irrigation system along the Tapi and Panjhra rivers managed diversion bandharas through democratic water councils that rotated water allocations equitably across crops, preventing soil waterlogging and water theft.

Colonial governance supplanted these local institutions with centralized bureaucratic management under the Public Works Department. The British constructed monumental perennial canal networks across the Indo-Gangetic basin, such as the Upper Ganga Canal engineered by Proby Cautley in 1854, the Western Yamuna Canal, and the Sirhind Canal. While these engineering works expanded irrigated acreage and prevented regional famines, they centralized water control in state irrigation engineers, imposed rigid warabandi rotational schedules, and allowed community tank infrastructure to fall into physical decay.

In the post-1970 era, the dominant hydraulic institution shifted decisively from public canals to decentralized private groundwater extraction. The diffusion of inexpensive diesel engines, subsidized rural electricity, and submersible pumps allowed individual farmers to bypass public canal bureaucracy and pump water directly from aquifers beneath their fields. Today, over eighty-five percent of net irrigated area added in India is supplied by private groundwater wells. While private tubewells delivered on-demand water reliability that drove high crop yields, they atomized water governance, dismantling collective management institutions and triggering a catastrophic tragedy of the commons in shared groundwater aquifers. Rebuilding participatory groundwater management institutions represents the paramount governance challenge of modern Indian agriculture.
