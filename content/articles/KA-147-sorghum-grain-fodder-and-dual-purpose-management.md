# Sorghum: Grain, Fodder and Dual-Purpose Management

Sorghum, designated botanically as Sorghum bicolor and celebrated across the Indian Deccan as jowar, occupies an extraordinary position in dryland agrarian ecosystems as the preeminent dual-purpose crop providing both food grain for human consumption and indispensable dry fodder for bovine livestock. Ranking as the fifth most important cereal globally, sorghum utilizes the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate malic enzyme type C4 photosynthetic mechanism, demonstrating remarkable drought resistance, extensive fibrous root penetration up to two point five meters deep, and the physiological capacity to enter temporary quiescence during mid-season moisture deficits before resuming vegetative growth upon rainfall resumption. Cultivated extensively across Maharashtra, northern Karnataka, Telangana, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan, sorghum serves as the economic and ecological bedrock of rainfed farming, where crop failure in conventional fine cereals would otherwise precipitate acute rural distress and widespread livestock destitution.

The agronomic management of sorghum in India is bifurcated into two distinct production regimes: rainy season (kharif) sorghum and post-rainy season (rabi) sorghum. Kharif sorghum is sown with the arrival of the southwest monsoon in late June or early July, typically utilizing high-yielding hybrids that mature in one hundred to one hundred and ten days. While kharif sorghum achieves high biomass and grain yields ranging from two point five to four tonnes per hectare, its harvest frequently coincides with late-monsoon September rains, triggering severe grain mold epidemics caused by fungal complexes including Fusarium moniliforme and Curvularia lunata. Grain mold blackens the caryopsis, degrades endosperm starches, and synthesizes dangerous mycotoxins, rendering kharif grain largely unsuited for premium human consumption and diverting it into poultry feed formulation, potable alcohol distillation, and industrial starch manufacturing. In sharp contrast, rabi sorghum represents a sophisticated dryland production system cultivated between late September and mid-October on deep, moisture-retentive black Vertisols relying entirely on conserved subsoil moisture accumulated during the receding monsoon. Dominated by historic landrace selections such as Maldandi M 35-1 alongside modern improved varieties like CSV-22R, Phule Anuradha, and Phule Revati, rabi sorghum matures under bright, rainless winter skies, producing spotless, lustrous, pearly-white grains renowned for their sweet taste, superior rolling quality for traditional unleavened flatbreads (bhakri), and high market valuation.

Dual-purpose management seeks to optimize both grain yield and the nutritional quality of the residual stover, known as kadbi, which forms the principal basal roughage for draft animals and dairy cattle throughout the arid semi-arid tracts. The genetic foundation of superior dual-purpose sorghum centers on the stay-green trait, a well-characterized physiological mechanism that delays foliar senescence during post-anthesis terminal drought. Plants expressing stay-green alleles maintain functional chlorophyll concentrations, continuous photosynthetic carbon assimilation, and active transpiration in upper canopy leaves even as available soil moisture drops below wilting point thresholds. Consequently, stay-green genotypes sustain uninterrupted grain filling without drawing down non-structural carbohydrates from lower stalk tissues, thereby preventing lodging and preserving high concentrations of soluble sugars and digestible dry matter within the mature stover. Stover harvested from stay-green cultivars exhibits higher crude protein content, elevated in-vitro organic matter digestibility, and reduced lignin content, supporting higher daily milk yields when fed to lactating murrah buffaloes and indigenous cattle.

Livestock safety in sorghum fodder management is critically governed by the biochemistry of dhurrin, an endogenous cyanogenic glucoside synthesized within young leaf tissues. Under normal physiological conditions, dhurrin is compartmentalized within cellular vacuoles, safely separated from cytosolic hydrolytic enzymes. However, when young, vegetative sorghum tissues are damaged by livestock mastication, mechanical trampling, severe drought stress, or frost, cellular compartmentalization breaks down, allowing dhurrin to encounter beta-glucosidase and hydroxynitrile lyases. This rapid enzymatic degradation generates hydrocyanic acid, also known as prussic acid, an extremely potent cellular toxin. When ruminants ingest stressed or immature sorghum containing prussic acid concentrations exceeding two hundred parts per million on a dry matter basis, hydrocyanic acid is rapidly absorbed into the bloodstream, where it binds irreversibly to ferric iron in cytochrome c oxidase, blocking the mitochondrial electron transport chain and causing swift death from cellular histotoxic anoxia within minutes. To completely avert prussic acid poisoning, farmers must strictly enforce agronomic guidelines: never graze cattle on stunted, moisture-stressed sorghum stands less than fifty centimeters in height or on young ratoons stimulated by light erratic showers, ensure that green fodder is harvested only after the crop reaches fifty percent flowering when dhurrin concentrations naturally decline to benign trace levels, and allow cut green fodder to air-dry and wilt in the sun for several hours, which facilitates the rapid dissipation of volatile hydrocyanic acid gas.

Beyond conventional grain and stover, sweet sorghum has emerged as an industrial dual-purpose marvel that aligns with national bio-economy and clean energy directives. Characterized by thick, juicy stalks containing ten to fifteen percent fermentable sugars, sweet sorghum produces normal grain panicles for human or animal nutrition while yielding forty to fifty tonnes of green stalks per hectare. Crushing these stalks at local cooperative sugar mills or decentralized crushing stations yields fermentable raw juice that converts efficiently into anhydrous bio-ethanol for fuel blending under national ethanol blending programs, leaving behind nutrient-rich bagasse that serves as cattle feed or co-generation boiler fuel. Cultivating sweet sorghum requires standard line sowing at sixty centimeters row spacing, a seed rate of seven to eight kilograms per hectare, and balanced fertilization of eighty kilograms of nitrogen, forty kilograms of phosphorus pentoxide, and forty kilograms of potassium oxide per hectare, providing an additional commercial revenue stream without competing against prime agricultural land devoted to basic food grains.

The production economics of dual-purpose sorghum reveal that stover often rivals or surpasses the monetary value of harvested grain, particularly in water-scarce rabi tracts. Frontline demonstration data compiled by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research and state agricultural universities indicate that cultivating rabi sorghum requires paid-out cash costs of sixteen thousand to twenty-two thousand rupees per hectare, covering land preparation, certified seed, basal fertilizer, and inter-cultivation weeding. A well-managed rabi crop yields one point five to two point two tonnes of premium food-grade grain per hectare, which commands prices between thirty-five hundred and four thousand five hundred rupees per quintal in urban consumption centers, generating fifty-two thousand to ninety-nine thousand rupees per hectare. Simultaneously, the crop generates three point five to five tonnes of dry kadbi per hectare, which commands premium livestock market rates of six hundred to one thousand rupees per quintal in dairy-dense western Maharashtra and northern Karnataka, delivering twenty-five thousand to forty-five thousand rupees per hectare in fodder revenue alone. The combined gross revenue frequently reaches eighty thousand to one hundred and thirty thousand rupees per hectare, netting clear profits of fifty thousand to eighty thousand rupees per hectare while maintaining household food self-reliance and insulating valuable dairy herds against recurrent summer feed famines.
