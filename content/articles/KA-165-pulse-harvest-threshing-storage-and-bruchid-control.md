# Pulse Harvest, Threshing, Storage and Bruchid Control

The preservation of physical integrity, biological viability, and economic value in harvested grain legumes depends entirely upon rigorous post-harvest operational discipline. While fine cereals such as wheat and paddy possess robust, silica-reinforced husks or hard flinty endosperms that provide natural resilience against mechanical threshing stress and storage pests, pulse grains are characterized by fragile seed coats, prominent embryonic axes, and protein-rich cotyledons that are acutely vulnerable to mechanical shattering, micro-fissuring, and catastrophic insect infestation. A producer may successfully navigate an entire growing season—managing fertilization, irrigation, and foliar diseases—only to forfeit thirty to fifty percent of total crop value through delayed harvesting, uncalibrated mechanical threshing, and unchecked storage devastation inflicted by pulse beetles. Establishing a profitable, resilient pulse enterprise requires an integrated post-harvest protocol that combines precise diurnal harvest scheduling, calibrated thresher cylinder mechanics, rigorous moisture reduction, and hermetic, chemical-free storage technologies that permanently neutralize storage insects.

The timing of pulse harvesting is governed by a delicate trade-off between physiological maturity and pod dehiscence, commonly known as pod shattering. Physiological maturity in pulses occurs when eighty to ninety percent of pods have lost their green photosynthetic pigmentation, transitioning to straw-yellow, golden-brown, or deep black coloration depending on the species, with internal seed moisture dropping to eighteen to twenty percent. Delaying harvest until the crop reaches complete field dryness exposes mature pods to thermal stress and desiccating winds, which trigger violent helical twisting of pod walls along their longitudinal sutures. In crops prone to explosive dehiscence, such as green gram, black gram, and cowpea, delayed harvesting can cause fifteen to twenty-five percent of the total grain harvest to shatter directly onto the field floor within forty-eight hours. To minimize shattering losses, manual harvesting using hand sickles or mechanical harvesting using tractor-mounted reapers must strictly be executed during the early morning hours between dawn and eight o'clock. During these early hours, ambient night humidity and dew deposition render the brittle pod walls pliable and resilient, preventing shattering during cutting and bundling. Harvested bundles should be transported immediately to clean threshing yards rather than left curing in dry fields where pods become brittle under intense afternoon sun.

Mechanical threshing operations must be meticulously calibrated to the delicate anatomical morphology of grain legumes. Traditional manual threshing—involving bullock treading or beating dried bundles with wooden flails—is not only physically exhausting and labor-intensive but also results in high grain breakage and severe contamination from field stones, soil clumps, and animal excreta. Modern mechanical multi-crop spike-tooth or rasp-bar threshers must be operated at significantly lower rotational drum speeds than those utilized for fine cereals. While wheat is threshed at cylinder speeds of eight hundred to one thousand revolutions per minute, pulse threshing demands that cylinder speed be throttled down to three hundred and fifty to four hundred and fifty revolutions per minute, accompanied by widening the concave clearance to fifteen to twenty millimeters. Operating threshers at excessive cylinder speeds subjects brittle pulse grains to violent mechanical impact, producing high percentages of cracked seed coats, split cotyledons, and pulverized flour. Even when grains do not split completely, microscopic fissures induced by high cylinder impact shatter the embryo axis, permanently destroying seed germination capacity and creating entry pathways for opportunistic storage fungi and saprophytic bacteria.

Once threshed, harvested pulse grains must undergo thorough, immediate drying to bring internal seed moisture down to safe storage thresholds. Freshly threshed grain typically contains thirteen to fifteen percent moisture, a level that promotes rapid cellular respiration, internal heating, and aggressive proliferation of storage molds. The harvested grain should be spread in thin layers, not exceeding five centimeters in depth, across clean canvas tarpaulins or concrete drying yards and exposed to bright sun drying for three to five consecutive days. Grains should be stirred frequently with wooden rakes to ensure uniform moisture evaporation. Sun drying must continue until seed moisture content drops strictly below nine to ten percent for long-term grain storage, or eight percent for high-value certified seed lots. Testing moisture using a calibrated electronic grain moisture meter or employing the traditional thumb-nail test—where a dry grain resists indentation and shatters with a crisp, audible snap when bitten—guarantees that the grain has entered physiological quiescence and will not support internal fungal spoilage.

The single most catastrophic biological hazard confronting stored pulses across India and tropical regions is the pulse beetle, encompassing several closely related species within the family Chrysomelidae, predominantly Callosobruchus maculatus (the cowpea bruchid) and Callosobruchus chinensis (the oriental pulse beetle). Bruchid infestation is particularly insidious because it frequently originates in the field prior to harvest. Adult female beetles fly from rural village storage godowns into neighboring pulse fields, laying tiny, translucent, dome-shaped eggs cemented individually onto the surface of developing green pods. Upon hatching, the microscopic larva burrows directly through the pod shell and penetrates the seed coat, entering the nutrient-rich cotyledons. The entrance hole is so minuscule that it heals over, leaving the harvested grain appearing completely sound and unblemished during threshing and initial bagging. However, once placed into warm storage conditions between twenty-five and thirty-five degrees Celsius, the grub feeds voraciously on internal starches and embryonic proteins, carving out an expansive internal feeding gallery. The larva pupates inside the hollowed grain, and upon metamorphosis, the adult beetle cuts a neat, circular exit hole—commonly referred to as a window—through the thin seed coat, emerging to mate and lay hundreds of eggs on adjacent grains. Because Callosobruchus completes a reproductive generation within twenty-one to twenty-eight days, a minor, undetectable field infestation can expand exponentially into millions of beetles within three to four months, reducing whole bags of pulse grain to empty, light-weight shells filled with powdery frass and toxic uric acid, destroying eighty percent of grain weight and completely annihilating germination viability.

Controlling bruchids requires moving beyond chemical fumigants toward integrated physical, biological, and hermetic storage technologies. In traditional commercial godowns, operators relied heavily on aluminum phosphide fumigation tablets to release toxic phosphine gas. However, extensive field surveys coordinated by the Indian Institute of Pulses Research reveal alarming levels of genetic phosphine resistance in native Indian Callosobruchus strains, rendering chemical fumigation increasingly unreliable. Furthermore, applying chemical insecticide dusts—such as malathion or synthetic pyrethroids—directly onto pulse grains intended for human consumption violates strict statutory Maximum Residue Limits enforced by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India, exposing consumers to neurotoxic residues. For smallholder farm-level storage, traditional botanical coatings provide safe, chemical-free protection: coating clean, dry pulse seeds with refined edible vegetable oils—such as mustard oil, neem oil, or coconut oil at five to ten milliliters per kilogram of grain—spreads a thin hydrophobic lipid film over the seed coat that blocks the aeropyle respiratory pores of bruchid eggs, asphyxiating developing embryos and preventing larval penetration. Mixing inert dusts like activated kaolin clay or diatomaceous earth at five grams per kilogram of grain physically scratches and absorbs the waxy epicuticular lipid layer of adult beetles, causing rapid death through physiological desiccation.

The modern gold standard of chemical-free pulse preservation is hermetic airtight storage, exemplified by Purdue Improved Crop Storage bags and multi-layer polyethylene hermetic liners. Hermetic technology operates on the foundational biological principle of respiratory modified atmospheres. Dry pulses are placed inside a heavy-duty outer woven polypropylene sack containing two independent, internal high-density polyethylene liners, which are tied off securely to form a gas-impermeable, airtight seal. Within this sealed environment, the normal aerobic respiration of the enclosed grains, seeds, and living bruchid insects rapidly consumes available oxygen while releasing carbon dioxide. Within five to seven days, internal oxygen concentrations plunge from ambient atmospheric levels of twenty-one percent down to lethal thresholds below three to five percent, while carbon dioxide concentrations rise. Under this severe hypoxic and hypercapnic environment, Callosobruchus insects at all developmental stages—eggs, larvae, pupae, and adults—suffer immediate respiratory arrest, metabolic collapse, and complete mortality within seven to ten days without altering the nutritional composition, flavor, or cooking qualities of the pulse. Hermetic storage bags maintain zero pest damage, preserve ninety percent seed germination vigor, and prevent moisture re-absorption for twelve to twenty-four months, empowering smallholders and commercial aggregators to store pulses safely and capture peak market prices during lean seasonal months.
