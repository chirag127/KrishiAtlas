# Harvesting, Threshing, Dehulling and Processing Requirements

The post-harvest transformation of raw field millets into clean, shelf-stable, and culinarily versatile food products represents one of the most technologically demanding and economically consequential phases in the nutri-cereal supply chain. For centuries, the widespread adoption of millets was severely constrained not by agronomic limitations in the field, but by the crushing physical drudgery associated with manual domestic processing. Women in rural households spent hours every day pounding small millets in stone mortars to fracture indigestible fibrous husks, followed by repetitive manual winnowing to separate edible kernels from abrasive chaff. In the modern era, breakthroughs in mechanical agricultural engineering, spearheaded by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research, the Indian Institute of Millets Research, and the Central Institute of Agricultural Engineering, have completely revolutionized this post-harvest landscape. Developing decentralized primary processing infrastructure that links precision harvesting, calibrated threshing, mechanical dehulling, and enzymatic stabilization has eliminated manual drudgery while unlocking commercial marketability for smallholder producers.

The foundational distinction governing post-harvest handling lies in the anatomical classification of millets into naked grains and husked grains. Naked grains, comprising sorghum, pearl millet, and finger millet, are free-threshing caryopses. In these species, the floral glumes, lemma, and palea do not adhere tightly to the seed pericarp, allowing the clean grain to separate cleanly from floral bracts during routine mechanical threshing, directly yielding food-grade grain ready for whole milling. In sharp contrast, husked grains, encompassing the minor millets—kodo, little, foxtail, proso, and barnyard millet—possess a heavily lignified, multi-layered fibrous outer husk containing five to eight percent silica that adheres tenaciously to the underlying caryopsis. This indigestible husk constitutes twenty to thirty-five percent of the total harvested grain weight and cannot be digested by human gastric enzymes. Consequently, small millets cannot simply be pulverized into whole meal; they must undergo specialized mechanical dehulling to strip away the abrasive outer husk while leaving the fragile, nutrient-dense bran and starchy endosperm intact.

Harvesting operations must be executed at precise physiological maturity to prevent catastrophic field losses from shattering, lodging, and bird predation. Physiological maturity in millets occurs when grain moisture drops to twenty to twenty-four percent, accompanied by the complete yellowing of foliage and the formation of a distinct dark melanin abscission layer at the hilar base of the caryopsis. Delaying harvest until the crop reaches field-dry levels of ten to twelve percent moisture exposes panicles to severe shattering caused by autumn winds, hail, and flocks of migrating granivorous birds, which can consume up to twenty percent of grain yield within forty-eight hours. Mechanized harvesting utilizes multi-crop reaper-binders or modified combine harvesters equipped with reel-speed governors and rasp-bar cylinders adjusted to lower peripheral speeds of four hundred to five hundred revolutions per minute. Threshing at excessive cylinder speeds shatters the delicate millet kernels, generating high proportions of cracked grain and fine flour that spoil rapidly during subsequent storage. Once threshed, harvested grain must be spread immediately onto clean, raised concrete threshing floors or tarpaulins for sun drying, driving internal moisture down to ten to twelve percent before bulk storage or commercial milling.

The mechanical processing train for husked minor millets begins with pre-cleaning and grading, operations that are strictly mandatory before raw grain can enter a dehulling machine. Raw millet lots delivered from rural farms contain high concentrations of abrasive field sand, micro-stones, weed seeds, immature kernels, and metallic debris. If fed directly into a dehuller, stones rapidly score and destroy internal rubber rollers and emery grinding surfaces, while sand contaminates the final food product. The standardized processing sequence utilizes a multi-deck vibratory grader equipped with aspirator fans and pneumatic destoners. The destoner separates dense stones from lighter millet grains utilizing fluidization principles, wherein an inclined oscillating screen and upward air current levitate the grains upward while dense stones migrate downward toward an ejector chute. Air classifiers simultaneously aspirate light dust, chaff, and unfilled glumes, ensuring that only uniform, clean, sound grains enter the dehuller.

Mechanical dehulling technologies for minor millets have evolved from abrasive emery cone mills toward high-efficiency centrifugal impact dehullers and rubber-roller shellers. In an abrasive emery mill, grains are stripped by friction between a rotating carborundum stone and a stationary perforated metal screen. While functional, abrasive mills cause high grain breakage and strip away the oil-rich aleurone layer if clearance is improperly calibrated. Centrifugal impact dehullers operate on a superior kinetic principle: grains are fed through a central impeller that accelerates them outward against a hardened urethane or ceramic impact ring, fracturing the brittle outer husk without crushing the resilient internal kernel. Advanced double-stage dehulling systems developed by the Indian Institute of Millets Research achieve eighty-five to ninety-two percent dehulling efficiency in a single continuous pass, with broken kernel percentages restricted below five to eight percent. Following impact, the mixture passes across an aspirating column that blows away light husk fragments, after which a gravity table separator cleanly divides dehulled polished millet rice from unhulled grains, recycling unhulled seeds back through the secondary dehulling chamber.

The formidable biochemical hurdle that confronts post-harvest millet processing is rapid hydrolytic and oxidative rancidity, particularly acute in pearl millet and minor millets. Unlike white rice or refined wheat, where the lipid-rich germ and bran are stripped away during industrial roller milling, millets possess a proportionally large embryonic germ that constitutes two to three percent of grain weight and contains five to seven percent crude fat, predominantly healthy polyunsaturated fatty acids such as linoleic and oleic acids. In the intact, undamaged seed, these cellular lipids are compartmentalized within spherosomes, isolated from active lipase enzymes residing in the aleurone layer. However, the moment the grain is mechanically dehulled, crushed, or ground into whole-grain flour, cellular membranes rupture, bringing triglycerides into direct physical contact with endogenous lipases. Within three to seven days at tropical ambient temperatures, lipases hydrolyze neutral fats into free fatty acids. Subsequently, lipoxygenase enzymes oxidize these free fatty acids into volatile hydroperoxides, which decompose into hexanal, ketones, and bitter aldehydes, imparting a repulsive, pungent, soapy odor and bitter taste that causes immediate consumer rejection.

Overcoming rancidity and establishing commercial shelf life requires the integration of hydrothermal stabilization, mild thermal inactivation, and barrier packaging technologies. Modern processing facilities subject clean, dehulled millets or whole grain to hydrothermal parboiling or dry heat treatment at one hundred to one hundred and ten degrees Celsius for thirty to sixty minutes. This thermal exposure denatures endogenous lipase and lipoxygenase enzymes, permanently arresting the enzymatic breakdown of fats without compromising starch functional properties or baking characteristics. Hydrothermally stabilized millet flour and semolina maintain free fatty acid levels below acceptable statutory limits for four to six months under standard ambient warehouse conditions. Concurrently, packaging stabilized millet grains and flours in nitrogen-flushed, vacuum-sealed pouches constructed from multi-layer co-extruded films or metallized polyester eliminates atmospheric oxygen and ambient humidity, fully safeguarding flavor, preserving essential micronutrients, and enabling smallholder agro-enterprises to distribute premium nutri-cereal products to national and global consumer markets.
