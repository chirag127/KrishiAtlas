Post-harvest storage, physiological shelf-life maintenance, and loss mitigation across root and tuber crops represent one of the most critical determinants of farm-gate profitability and food system stability in Indian agriculture. Generating tens of millions of tonnes of nutrient-dense storage biomass annually across potato, sweet potato, cassava, elephant foot yam, and edible yams, the root and tuber sector is plagued by devastating post-harvest quantitative losses that regularly consume twenty to thirty-five percent of total physical yield between excavation and retail consumption. Unlike dry cereal grains or oilseeds that can be sun-dried to low equilibrium moisture levels below twelve percent to enter profound metabolic stasis, subterranean storage organs are living, hydrated biological structures containing seventy to eighty-five percent water. Harvested roots and tubers continue active cellular respiration, transpire moisture through thin periderms, suffer opportunistic microbial rots through mechanical harvest wounds, and undergo physiological dormancy release, triggering premature sprouting that mobilizes stored starches and destroys commercial processing and culinary grade. Preserving tuber quality and capturing lucrative counter-seasonal market prices requires an intimate understanding of wound-healing curing thermodynamics, sprout-inhibition chemistry, and modern cold-chain engineering.

The physiological lifecycle of a harvested tuber or corm begins in a state of natural dormancy, an evolutionary survival mechanism designed to suspend bud growth during unfavorable dry or cold seasons. Fungal and plant physiologists categorize tuber dormancy into endodormancy, where internal biochemical and hormonal balances—dominated by high concentrations of endogenous abscisic acid and low levels of gibberellins and cytokinins—prevent bud sprout emergence even under optimal temperature and moisture conditions; and ecodormancy, where sprout growth is arrested purely by external environmental constraints, such as severe cold storage below four degrees Celsius. The natural duration of endodormancy varies widely across botanical taxa: Irish potato cultivars exhibit endodormancy spanning six to ten weeks post-harvest; elephant foot yam corms enter prolonged subterranean dormancy lasting three to four months; whereas cassava tubers possess virtually zero physiological dormancy, exhibiting wound-induced cellular breakdown within forty-eight hours of excavation.

When tuber endodormancy breaks, a profound physiological transformation occurs within the storage parenchyma. Dormant buds located at eyes or collars perceive ambient warmth and moisture, triggering a surge in endogenous gibberellin synthesis that activates alpha-amylase and invertase enzymes. These hydrolytic enzymes aggressively degrade stored, insoluble starch granules into soluble reducing sugars, predominantly glucose and fructose, which are translocated to fuel the rapid elongation of vegetative sprouts. Sprout development represents a catastrophic loss of commercial value: the expanding sprouts consume internal moisture and carbohydrates, causing the parent tuber to lose turgor pressure, shrivel into a wrinkled, leathery mass, and lose up to twenty percent of its fresh physical weight within weeks. In Irish potatoes, sprouting is accompanied by the de-novo synthesis of bitter, toxic steroidal glycoalkaloids, predominantly alpha-solanine and alpha-chaconine, concentrated around the eyes and greening periderm; in food-processing potatoes, elevated reducing sugars undergo non-enzymatic Maillard browning reactions during frying, producing black, bitter chips containing hazardous levels of carcinogenic acrylamide, leading to total rejection by industrial snack manufacturers.

Preventing storage deterioration begins with the foundational operation of wound curing and suberization. During harvesting, heavy tractor diggers, spades, and rough manual handling inevitably cause minor scrapes, cuts, bruises, and skinning abrasions across the delicate tuber periderm. In fresh, un-cured storage organs, these unsealed wounds serve as open biological portals through which moisture rapidly evaporates, while opportunistic wound pathogens—preeminently soft-rot fungi like Rhizopus stolonifer and Fusarium oxysporum, alongside pectolytic bacteria such as Pectobacterium carotovorum—rapidly penetrate the interior, liquefying the storage parenchyma into foul-smelling slime within days.

Commercial curing eliminates this vulnerability by inducing natural physiological wound repair. Freshly harvested potatoes, sweet potatoes, and yams are subjected to a specific microclimatic regime immediately post-harvest: sweet potatoes and tropical yams are held at warm temperatures between twenty-eight and thirty-two degrees Celsius with eighty-five to ninety percent relative humidity for four to seven days, while Irish potatoes are cured at fifteen to twenty degrees Celsius with ninety percent relative humidity for ten to fourteen days. Under these conditions, the exposed parenchymal cells beneath the cut surface rapidly synthesize and deposit suberin, a complex, hydrophobic waxy biopolymer that impregnates cell walls, forming a temporary suberin seal within forty-eight hours. Over the subsequent five days, an active wound phellogen meristem initiates underneath the suberized layer, dividing to form multiple layers of new, durable, corky periderm cells. This newly regenerated cork skin completely seals the wound against further transpirational water loss and establishes an impenetrable physical barrier against bacterial and fungal spore penetration, extending potential storage longevity from weeks to months.

Long-term commercial storage infrastructure is split between low-cost on-farm evaporative structures and refrigerated industrial cold stores. For smallholders and marginal farmers lacking capital access to commercial refrigeration, the Zero Energy Cool Chamber, developed by the Indian Agricultural Research Institute, provides a low-cost, decentralized storage solution. Constructed from a double-brick masonry enclosure filled with clean river sand kept continuously saturated with water, the chamber operates via thermodynamic evaporative cooling. As water evaporates from the wet sand, it absorbs latent heat from the interior, depressing internal storage temperatures by ten to fifteen degrees Celsius below ambient summer heat while maintaining relative humidity above ninety percent, successfully extending the on-farm storage life of elephant foot yam, sweet potatoes, and table potatoes by six to eight weeks without a single watt of electricity.

In large-scale commercial potato supply chains, produce is stored in massive, multi-tiered industrial refrigerated cold stores. Operating protocols are governed strictly by the intended market destination of the tuber. Seed potatoes meant for subsequent crop propagation are held at cold temperatures of two to four degrees Celsius with ninety to ninety-five percent relative humidity; these near-freezing temperatures completely arrest bud respiration and suppress sprouting indefinitely without requiring chemical sprout inhibitors. However, storing processing or table potatoes at two to four degrees Celsius triggers a devastating physiological defect known as cold-induced sweetening, where low temperatures stimulate vacuolar invertase enzymes to rapidly convert starch into reducing sugars, ruining frying quality.

Consequently, commercial processing potatoes are stored at warmer, intermediate temperatures of eight to twelve degrees Celsius to prevent cold sweetening. However, because these warmer temperatures naturally break tuber dormancy and promote vigorous sprouting, facilities must apply chemical sprout suppressants. The global and Indian industry standard is chlorpropham, commonly abbreviated as CIPC. Applied inside sealed cold store rooms as a hot thermal aerosol fog at a rate of twenty to thirty grams per tonne of tubers when dormancy begins to break, CIPC acts as a mitotic spindle inhibitor, permanently arresting cell division in the meristematic eye buds without affecting internal starch quality. For organic and residue-conscious markets, bio-rational alternatives—such as aerosolized spearmint oil, peppermint oil, or clove oil whose active terpene carvone physically scorches tender sprout tips—provide clean sprout suppression. By integrating prompt post-harvest curing, decentralized evaporative cooling, end-use specific temperature management, and calibrated sprout suppression, commercial tuber enterprises dramatically curtail post-harvest losses and capture premium year-round market valuations.
